\abstract{Automatic graders for programming courses, build farms and serverless platforms run other people's jobs on $k$ servers without interruption, each job under a time limit $L$. Ordering the queue by predicted running time shortens most waits, but one wrong prediction can leave one job far behind. We ask what such a service can promise to every job when the predictions are arbitrary. We prove that under any non-preemptive work-conserving policy, on every arrival sequence, a job's delay relative to first-come first-served (FCFS) equals the net work that overtook it divided by $k$, to within $(2-2/k)L$, and the constant is exact. A budget on overtaken work is therefore sufficient for a per-job guarantee and necessary up to an additive $O(kL)$; a count of overtakes is not. A wrapper that enforces the budget bounds every job's wait against FCFS for arbitrary arrivals, predictions and base policies, with the exact constant $(3-2/k)L$. We evaluate on logs from two judging platforms, a serverless trace, two continuous-integration pools and a compute farm. With scores that use only results available in the replay, a \devnum{600}-second guard closes \devnum{0.672} of the FCFS-to-shortest-job-first tail-wait gap at the busiest development load, interval \devnum{[0.602, 0.743]}.}
