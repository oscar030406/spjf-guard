\abstract{Services that grade students' programs, run cloud functions or test software run many users' jobs on $k$ shared servers without interruption, each under a time limit $L$. Ordering the queue by predicted running time shortens most waits but can hold a job back for a long time. What can every job be promised when predictions may be arbitrarily wrong? Under any work-conserving, non-preemptive rule, a job's extra wait relative to first-come first-served (FCFS) equals the net running time of the jobs that overtook it, divided by $k$, up to an error of $(2-2/k)L$ that cannot be improved. Limiting that running time is therefore sufficient for a per-job promise and, up to an additive $O(kL)$, necessary. A guard enforcing such a limit bounds every job's wait against FCFS for any arrivals, predictions and underlying policy, with exact constant $(3-2/k)L$, plus $L+\delta$ when completion reports arrive up to $\delta$ late. On a university grading log at peak load, scoring each job from results already returned to its own class, a guard promising 600 seconds keeps \devnum{0.705} of the reduction in 99th-percentile wait that shortest-job-first achieves over FCFS, and \sealednum{0.894} on terms held back until the method was frozen.}
