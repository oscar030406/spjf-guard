\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{The queue is constructed, and the replay is open-loop.} CodeBench gives each student
a separate container \cite{codebench_home}, so its logs do not record waiting in a shared
queue \cite{codebench_dataset}. We use these logs as a record of demand, with reconstructed
arrival times and recorded running times. The pool, the queue and the scheduler are ours,
and nothing here describes waiting on that platform. A fixed pool of grading machines behind
one queue is a standard deployment \cite{domjudge_manual,peveler2019sandbox}, and on the
same demand \devnum{14} servers replace \devnum{1{,}636} concurrent containers
\cite{smith1981pooling} (Supplementary Section~S2.3). Arrivals are replayed at their
recorded times, so a scheduler is neither rewarded with the work it would attract nor
punished for the users it would drive away. Replaying each student's recorded delay between
one result and the next submission (Section~\ref{sec:exp_sens}) restores one part of that
feedback and does not reverse the benefit. It restores neither abandonment nor changes in
what students submit, however, and the recorded delays come from a platform on which no
student waited. Our results are therefore conditional on a fixed amount of offered work,
and Supplementary Section~S6.3 constructs feedback under which a scheduling advantage
disappears or reverses. We raise the load by overlaying whole class-terms aligned on
weekday and clock time, rather than by compressing the gaps between arrivals
\cite{harcholbalter2003sizebased}, which would also compress the daily cycle
\cite{feitelson2021resampling}.

\textbf{The guarantee is written in units of $L$, and per job.} Every additive constant in
Section~\ref{sec:theory} is a multiple of the time limit, apart from the report delay
$\delta$ and the arrival burst $\sigma$, and the guard's constant $(3-2/k)L$ cannot be
improved. No wrapper that reorders at all can guarantee less than $L$ around every base
policy (Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}). Stopping long jobs and restarting them in a second
tier, as task assignment by guessing size does \cite{harcholbalter2002tags}, does not
escape this floor on a shared pool. The stopped work is lost rather than overtaken, and the
second tier is again non-preemptive in blocks of $L$ (Supplementary Section~S7.5).
Charging a cap known at arrival lowers the constant by exactly $L$ and leaves the floor.
Because the guarantee bounds each job, it also bounds each percentile,
$Q_\alpha(W_P) \le Q_\alpha(W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}) + G$. It does not ensure that a percentile
improves, and under corrupted scores a guarded run had a worse
$\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ than FCFS (Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}). Where the FCFS tail is
already below about $3L$, the bound is coarser than the waits it bounds, which is the case
for four of the six workloads (Section~\ref{sec:exp_cross}).

\textbf{Development results and the sealed terms.} We explored the guard family and an
early parameter grid on the overlay the tables report, and only then searched the
pre-stated grids on validation overlays that exclude the evaluation term. The selection is
thus validation-only within a family that is not. The worst-cell rule is sensitive to which
cells it sees, and the five overlays are week-shifts of the same class-terms rather than
independent samples. Resampling whole class-terms was run under the original protocol only,
so the online figures carry intervals from resampled weeks, which do not cover the choice of
classes and terms. Moreover, the shape selected on \devnum{15} cells with $k \ge 4$ does
not carry over to a single server (Supplementary Section~S3.14). We therefore do not call
the parameters validated. The guarantee does not depend on them, and only the benefit does.
The sealed terms were opened once, after the method, parameters and metric were frozen
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_sealed}). They come from the same platform and pass through the same
constructed queue, so they test the frozen choices on new classes and terms but not on
another service.

\textbf{Information, costs, and what was not studied.} The guard's parameters were selected
under the original protocol. Of the comparisons after Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}, only
the \devnum{24} main policies of Sections~\ref{sec:exp_rank} and~\ref{sec:exp_guard} were
also replayed under the online one (Supplementary Section~S11). Every other comparison there,
and the queue comparison that chose the predictor (Section~\ref{sec:pred_comparison}), rests
on the optimistic original protocol. The online protocol
takes the results of other classes and terms at their recorded times. The recorded costs are wall-clock durations that already
contain some contention, and the \devnum{60}-second limit is inferred from the data rather
than documented. ACcoding arrival times are drawn because the released tables omit them
(Supplementary Section~S2.1). Preemption is outside the model. The wrapper applies
unchanged to a learned or reinforcement-learned base policy, which we did not test.
Capacity is fixed within each replay, and the capacity sweep is a sensitivity, not a
planning study. Computing the features and running the predictor are charged only in a
sensitivity run, where they move the gap closed by less than \devnum{0.001}
(Section~\ref{sec:prediction}). We ran a physical service but no production deployment, and
we measured no user response. Nor did we combine the guard with an existing scheduler such
as the serverless prototype of Fu et al.\ \cite{fu2024alps}, which has no proved per-job
bound.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A service that runs many users' jobs on a few shared servers can order its queue by
predicted running time and still guarantee every job a wait no more than $G$ seconds longer
than in arrival order, whatever the predictions are. This guarantee follows from one
identity. A job's extra wait relative to FCFS equals the net running time of the jobs that
overtook it, divided by the number of servers, up to at most $(2-2/k)L$. Limiting that
overtaken work is therefore sufficient for a per-job guarantee and, up to an additive
$O(kL)$, necessary. The guard enforces such a limit with the exact constant $(3-2/k)L$, and
its guarantee survives completion reports that arrive up to $\delta$ late at a cost of
$L + \delta$. A maximum waiting time gives a guarantee of the same form. Near saturation
and at tight bounds, however, it keeps much less of the benefit, because it charges a job
for all the work ahead of it and not only for the jobs that pass it. Counting overtakes
gives the same guarantee only at a charge of $L$ per pass, which on the main log is
\devnum{150} times the mean running time.

For the grading system that opened this paper, the answer to a student whose submission sat
behind a looping one is now a number. At the busiest load of the recorded course demand,
with every score computed from the results available when its job arrives, a guard with
$G = \devnum{600}$ s keeps \devnum{0.705} of the reduction in 99th-percentile wait before
deadlines that SJF achieves over FCFS. Unguarded ordering keeps \devnum{0.864}, and with the
guard no job exceeds its bound. On three terms held back until the method was frozen, the
same guard keeps \sealednum{0.894} and unguarded ordering \sealednum{0.936}. In seconds,
the 99th-percentile wait of the submissions made in the 24 hours before a deadline falls
from \devnum{273.5} s under FCFS to \devnum{111.2} s with the guard, and from
\sealednum{187.20} s to \sealednum{47.22} s on the held-back terms. No job waits more than
\devnum{600} s beyond its FCFS wait, where unguarded ordering delayed one job by
\devnum{5{,}812.7} s. The scores come from gradient-boosted trees on each
student's and each task's history, chosen on queueing outcomes rather than prediction
accuracy. A heterogeneous graph network over students, tasks, assignments and cohorts
has a rank correlation between the two tree models' over all \devnum{40{,}844} test submissions and was not
run through the queue. On the \devnum{1{,}092} submissions to tasks with fewer than five
earlier results, the situation that a newly released assignment creates, it ranks jobs
better than the trees by \devnum{0.146} in rank correlation, and the same network without
edges does so by \devnum{0.142}, so the gain there comes from the neural model of each
job's own features. The demand is constructed in both evaluations, and the benefit depends
on capacity: it appeared only for $k = 4$ to $12$.

Two questions remain open. Under the executed-work convention of
Section~\ref{sec:conventions}, the lower constant of the identity is known only to lie in
$[(k-1)L, 2(k-1)L]$. We also do not know whether some wrapper overrides the base policy
strictly less often than Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} while guaranteeing the same $G$.
