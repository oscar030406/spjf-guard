\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The shared pool is our design.}
CodeBench gives each logged-in student a private container, so on the real platform these
logs do not identify a shared judging queue \cite{codebench_dataset}: we use them as a
demand trace, with reconstructed arrivals and recorded execution durations, while the
server pool, the queue and the scheduler are ours, and nothing here is a statement about
waiting on the real platform. What supports the design is that a fixed pool of judge
hosts on one queue is a standard deployment \cite{domjudge_manual,peveler2019sandbox} and
that pooling replaces \devnum{1{,}636} concurrent containers with \devnum{14} servers on
the same demand \cite{smith1981pooling}. Section~\ref{sec:data} and Supplementary
Section~S2.3 give that evidence and say why the container ratio belongs to the overlay
and to no deployment and is not a cost saving, the container also serving the student's
editing session; Supplementary Section~S6.2 says why the physical timed-work run, whose
rule-conformance checks coexist with a worse guarded p99 and large per-job deviations
from the ideal simulator, does not validate transport of submitted-program costs.

\paragraph{Open-loop replay.}
Arrivals come from the log at their recorded instants and do not react to our scheduler,
so a scheduler is neither rewarded with the work it would attract nor punished by the
users it would drive away \cite{feitelson2021resampling,shmueli2009simulation}. We cannot
restore that feedback, our logs carrying no session boundaries, no think times and no
resubmission semantics, and its direction is not identified: the results are conditional
on fixed offered work rather than lower bounds on closed-loop benefit, and the exact
feedback counterexample in Supplementary Section~S6.3 shows a scheduling advantage
disappearing in one construction and reversing in another. That section also states
Feitelson's objection in his own words and the productivity distinction Shmueli and
Feitelson draw. Replaying into a differently sized pool is established practice
\cite{harcholbalter2003sizebased}, and the width coupling that distorts parallel-job
replays does not arise here, a job occupying one server whatever $k$ is. One choice is
deliberate: we raise load by superposing whole class-semesters aligned on weekday and
clock time, rather than compressing inter-arrival times, which shrinks the daily load
cycle along with the gaps \cite{feitelson2021resampling}.

\paragraph{The guarantee is denominated in $L$.}
Every constant in Section~\ref{sec:theory} is a multiple of the per-job time
limit. The additive constant $(3-2/k)L$ is an exact supremum and no
artefact of the proof, it grows with $k$ from $L$ at a single server towards
$3L$, and a promise that is to hold around every base policy cannot be less than
$L$ unless the wrapper gives up reordering altogether: whenever the budget is positive
for the waiting jobs at the first dispatch epoch there are a base policy and an input
on which some job's excess is at least $L$, and the only budget rules of this family
that escape are those that make the wrapper first-come first-served itself
(Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}). So no mechanism of this family that reorders at all
can promise less than $\Omega(L)$.
The obvious escape from the $\Omega(L)$ floor is kill-and-restart tiering, and it fails.
It is TAGS \cite{harcholbalter2002tags} with the dedicated hosts removed, and removing
them is what breaks it: the kill becomes terminal, so the excess it leaves is wasted work
rather than overtaking and no budget removes it, while the light-job constant does not
improve either, a second-tier piece being still a non-preemptive block of length $L$.
Supplementary Section~S7.5 gives both arguments with the constant we measure.

\paragraph{Two applicability conditions, each with traces that fail it.}
The first asks whether the uniform certificate is informative at the waits that matter,
and four of the original six workloads fail it: their first-come first-served p99 wait
runs from \devnum{0.021} to \devnum{1.78} multiples of $L$, against the additive term of
about \devnum{3}$L$ in the certificate. The Intel Netbatch compute farm
\cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch} is the extreme, with a service cap of
\devnum{5.7} days against a worst-window p99 wait of \devnum{18.7} hours, so that the
uniform additive term is about \devnum{17} days, the guard never fires at any positive
budget tested, and predicted-cost ordering makes the p99 wait worse than first-come
first-served there under sustained overload. This does not establish impossibility below
that term for a restricted base policy or workload, and the guard results we report on
the four workloads that fail the condition measure the ordering mechanism there. The
second is an empirical opportunity screen and not a theorem assumption or a necessary
condition for a benefit: on the two Firefox CI hardware pools
\cite{taskcluster_docs,treeherder_docs} the longest \devnum{1\%} of jobs carry
\devnum{3.4\%} and \devnum{5.2\%} of the work, at coefficients of variation \devnum{0.61}
and \devnum{0.87}, and there even true-size ordering makes the p99 wait worse than
first-come first-served on three of the four windows we ran.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_cross} gives both conditions in numbers, trace by trace.

\paragraph{The guarantee is per job, not per quantile.}
On a fixed job subset, the pointwise bound implies
$Q_\alpha(W_P)\le Q_\alpha(W_{\mathrm{FCFS}})+G$ by monotonicity of quantiles.
It does not imply an improvement in that percentile. A
guarded run can have a worse deadline-window p99 than the baseline its guarantee
is stated against, by as much as the promise itself, and Supplementary Table~S7
shows it doing so under two of the three corrupted scores at the busiest load
while the worst single job stays inside the promise.

\paragraph{Development data, and what is still sealed.}
Every CodeBench number in this paper is development data, and none of it is out of
sample: the guard family and an early parameter grid were explored on the same overlay
the tables report, before the three pre-stated grids were searched on validation overlays
that exclude the evaluation semester, so the selection is validation-only inside a family
that is not. The rule is a worst-cell rule and is sensitive to which validation cells it
sees --- at the middle promise, dropping the one cell that decides the choice moves it to
a different point of the same grid --- and the five overlays are week-shifts of the same
class-terms rather than five independent tests, so we do not describe any of these
parameters as validated. The guarantee does not depend on the parameters being well
chosen; only the benefit does. The sealed semesters will be opened once, after the
method, the parameters and the primary metric have been frozen, and their numbers
reported separately; that evaluation is not completed here, and the second judging
platform's sealed id block and the corroborating trace's sealed year stay shut, the
frozen pipeline covering the main trace alone (Section~\ref{sec:sealed}).

\paragraph{The budget shape is selected on one set of cells, and does not always
transfer.} The promise holds for any budget under the cap, so nothing about the
guarantee depends on the shape; what the shape decides is how much of the base policy's
benefit survives, and that we select on \devnum{15} multi-server validation cells, which
are the whole evidence we have for it. The single-server configuration shows the limit of
that evidence: at $G = \devnum{1200}$ s the queue-length shape wins the joint comparison
on those cells and closes \devnum{0.701} of the gap at $k = 1$, where the age-relative
shape closes \devnum{0.895}, while at the two tighter promises the same choice does
transfer. We report the number the pre-stated rule produces and do not re-tune on the
configuration that exposes it; an operator whose pool is much smaller than the validation
pool should re-run the selection rather than take our shape. The three shapes also differ
in where the harm falls, so which is preferable depends on a judgement about jobs
first-come first-served would have started at once, and that judgement is the operator's.

\paragraph{What the recorded costs mean.}
The cost we replay is the platform's recorded execution time, a wall-clock duration for
one evaluation that includes interpreter start-up, and part of it is contention we then
re-impose inside the pool: between \devnum{38\%} and \devnum{79\%} of jobs longer than a
second end in the same second as another block of that student. The observed
\devnum{60}-second regime is not documentation of an enforced timeout, and how the
platform enforces such a limit is undocumented in the sources we found; we wrote to the
maintainers about both points and had no answer. ACcoding \cite{accoding2024} records no
submission timestamps, so its arrival process is synthetic. The Firefox CI checks fit an
effective capacity and a setup term on one chronological part of each window and test on
the other; out of sample the mean wait, the per-hour profile and the per-job ordering
survive and the 90th percentile does not, so the simulated distribution is too steep in
its upper middle. Supplementary Section~S2.2 gives that refit with its sharpness and
circularity checks and the window lengths the split is taken inside.

\paragraph{History availability and the recorded-queue counterexample.}
Original-clock scores may use outcomes that are still pending in the replay, and the
conservative headline reduces history and changes fitted models as well as release delay,
so its difference from the original score does not identify a queue-delay effect alone;
the pending exact variant certifies availability for its final same-copy replay, not an
online fixed point or a deployment with globally shared copied-user state
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}).

LPC-EGEE provides both recorded waits and concentrated costs, but the fitted FCFS
approximation underestimates chronological-test mean waits by \devnum{24\%} and
\devnum{39\%} on its two physical partitions, and the work-budget guards never fire at
the tested promises. We therefore treat its policy replays as conditional counterfactuals
rather than validated estimates of deployment benefit; Supplementary Section~S6.4 gives
the correlations, what the archive says about its ordering, and the concurrency ceilings
and middleware the replay omits.

\paragraph{Not studied.}
Preemption is outside the model, so nothing here applies to a system that can checkpoint
or suspend. The wrapper applies unchanged to a learned or reinforcement-learned base
policy, its bound assuming nothing about the base, but we did not test one. Capacity is
fixed within each replay, and the capacity sweep is a sensitivity to that choice rather
than an estimate of the original platform's capacity or a capacity-planning study. The
reported runs compare policies at a common set of enqueue instants and do not charge the
cost of computing features and running the predictor; Section~\ref{sec:prediction}
measures that cost and charges it in a sensitivity, where it moves the closed gap by less
than \devnum{0.001} at every load, so what is missing is not the latency but the rest of
an end-to-end system. The local timed-work service was executed, but there was no
prospective production deployment or measurement of user feedback. A deployed serverless
scheduler of the kind this wrapper is meant for already exists, ALPS \cite{fu2024alps}
being a mechanism without a proved per-job bound.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We asked whether a non-preemptive shared execution service can be reordered by
predicted cost without exposing any single job to an unbounded wait. What the
evidence supports is that work is the currency the excess is written in. A
job's delay relative to first-come first-served equals its net
overtaken work divided by the number of servers, up to a constant multiple of
the time limit, and a budget on that quantity is sufficient and necessary for a
per-job guarantee. Counting overtakes is not necessary for such a guarantee, and
buys one only at the worst-case charge of the time limit per pass, which on our
traces overstates the work that actually passes by one to two orders of
magnitude. The wrapper built on it holds every job to a promise the
operator states in seconds, whatever the predictor does. Under conservative
visibility, Guard($\devnum{600}$) closes $\devnum{0.429}$ of the reference gap at the
busiest load, against $\devnum{0.670}$ for unguarded ordering. These are development
results conditional on the constructed demand and information protocol.
Four of the original six workloads have tail waits too short relative to $L$ for the
uniform certificate to be useful; concentration of job costs is an empirical
opportunity for reordering, not a necessity claim.

Two questions are left open. The identity is stated in the dispatch-sequence
currency, where the constant is exactly determined in both directions; under the
executed-work convention the upward constant is unchanged and the downward one is
known only to lie in $((k-1)L, 2(k-1)L]$, and closing it would make the two
conventions interchangeable. And Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} is not claimed to be the
least restrictive rule carrying its guarantee: whether some wrapper overrides the
base policy strictly less often and still promises $G$ is a question we have not
settled in either direction.
