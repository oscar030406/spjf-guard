\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

Three things here could be wrong in ways the experiments would not show. The queue we
replay never existed. The scores we rank by were fitted with knowledge of later events
that we only partly remove. And the guarantee is measured in $L$. We take them in turn,
and the smaller threats after them.

\paragraph{The shared pool is our design.}
CodeBench creates a separate container for each student \cite{codebench_home}, so on the real platform
these logs do not identify a shared judging queue \cite{codebench_dataset}. We use
them as a demand trace, with reconstructed arrivals and recorded execution durations.
The server pool, the queue and the scheduler are ours, and nothing here is a statement
about waiting on the real platform. What supports the design is that a fixed pool of
judge hosts on one queue is a standard deployment
\cite{domjudge_manual,peveler2019sandbox}, and that pooling replaces \devnum{1{,}636}
concurrent containers with \devnum{14} servers on the same demand
\cite{smith1981pooling}. Section~\ref{sec:data} and Supplementary Section~S2.3 give
that evidence. They also say why the container ratio belongs to the overlay and to no
deployment, and why it is not a cost saving, since the container also serves the
student's editing session. The physical timed-work run does not validate transport of
submitted-program costs either: its rule-conformance checks sit beside a worse
guarded p99 and with large per-job deviations from the ideal simulator (Supplementary
Section~S6.2).

\paragraph{Open-loop replay.}
Arrivals come from the log at their recorded instants and do not react to our
scheduler. A scheduler is therefore neither rewarded with the work it would attract
nor punished by the users it would drive away
\cite{feitelson2021resampling,shmueli2009simulation}. One component of that feedback
is in the logs, which identify each submission's author and its position in that
student's sequence. The offset between one result and the next submission is a
recorded think time: it is non-negative for \devnum{98.38\%} of consecutive pairs, its
median is \devnum{123.5} s, and its Spearman correlation with the previous job's cost
is \devnum{0.015}. We replayed the traces a second time with each submission released
after its predecessor's result plus that offset. The deadline-window p99 and the mean wait both shorten, and the benefit
reverses in none of the nine development cells: on their original-clock scores
Guard(\devnum{600}) closes \devnum{0.734}, \devnum{0.827} and \devnum{0.800} of the
FCFS-to-SJF deadline-window gap at the three loads under the recorded instants, and
\devnum{0.825}, \devnum{0.809} and \devnum{0.810} under gating (Supplementary
Section~S6.5). That replay does not restore abandonment or content feedback, and the
recorded think times come from a platform on which no student ever waited. So the
direction of what is left is still not identified. The results are conditional on
fixed offered work rather than lower bounds on closed-loop benefit, and the exact
feedback counterexample in Supplementary Section~S6.3 shows a scheduling advantage
disappearing in one construction and reversing in another. That section also states
Feitelson's objection in his own words and the productivity distinction Shmueli and
Feitelson draw. The width coupling that distorts parallel-job replays in a pool of
another size does not arise here, because a job occupies one server whatever $k$ is.
One choice is deliberate. Trace-driven studies often raise the load by compressing
inter-arrival times \cite{harcholbalter2003sizebased}, which shrinks the daily load
cycle along with the gaps \cite{feitelson2021resampling}. We raise it instead by
overlaying whole class-semesters aligned on weekday and clock time.

\paragraph{The guarantee is measured in $L$.}
Every additive constant in Section~\ref{sec:theory} is a multiple of the per-job time
limit, apart from the report delay $\delta$ and the arrival burst $\sigma$.
The additive constant $(3-2/k)L$ is an exact supremum, not an artefact of the proof.
It grows with $k$, from $L$ at a single server towards $3L$. A promise that is to hold
around every base policy cannot be less than $L$ unless the wrapper gives up
reordering altogether. The reason is simple. Whenever the budget is positive for the
waiting jobs at the first dispatch epoch, there are a base policy and an input on which
some job's excess is at least $L$. The only budget rules of this family that escape
are the ones that make the wrapper first-come first-served itself
(Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}). So no mechanism of this family that reorders at all
can promise less than $\Omega(L)$. The obvious escape from that floor is
kill-and-restart tiering, and it fails. It is TAGS \cite{harcholbalter2002tags} with
the dedicated hosts removed, and removing them is what breaks it. The kill becomes
terminal, so the excess it leaves is wasted work rather than overtaking, and no budget
removes it. The light-job constant does not improve either, because a second-tier
piece is still a non-preemptive block of length $L$. Supplementary Section~S7.5 gives
both arguments with the constant we measure. When the service enforces a per-job cap
that is known at arrival, charging the cap at dispatch and refunding it at completion
lowers the constant by exactly $L$ (Section~\ref{sec:theory}); it does not remove the
floor.

\paragraph{Two applicability conditions, each with traces that fail it.}
The first condition asks whether the uniform certificate is informative at the waits
that matter, and the second is an empirical opportunity screen rather than a theorem
assumption. Four of the original six workloads fail the first, among them the Intel
Netbatch compute farm \cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch}, whose service cap of
\devnum{5.7} days is the largest, and the two Firefox CI pools \cite{taskcluster_docs,treeherder_docs} fail
the second. Section~\ref{sec:exp_cross} gives both conditions in numbers, trace by
trace, and Supplementary Section~S6.8 says what failing either does and does not
establish.

\paragraph{The guarantee is per job, not per quantile.}
On a fixed set of jobs, the pointwise bound implies
$Q_\alpha(W_P)\le Q_\alpha(W_{\mathrm{FCFS}})+G$ by monotonicity of quantiles. It does
not imply an improvement in that percentile. A guarded run can have a worse
deadline-window p99 than the baseline its guarantee is stated against, by as much as
the promise itself. Supplementary Table~S8 shows it doing so under two of the three
corrupted scores at the busiest load, while the worst single job stays inside the
promise.

\paragraph{Development data, and what is still sealed.}
Every CodeBench number in this paper is development data, and none of it is out of
sample. We explored the guard family and an early parameter grid on the same overlay
the tables report, and only then searched the three pre-stated grids on validation
overlays that exclude the evaluation semester. The selection is therefore
validation-only inside a family that is not. The rule is a worst-cell rule and is
sensitive to which validation cells it sees: at the middle promise, dropping the one
cell that decides the choice moves it to a different point of the same grid. The five
overlays are week-shifts of the same class-terms rather than five independent tests.
For both reasons we do not describe any of these parameters as validated. The
guarantee does not depend on the parameters being well chosen; only the benefit does.
The sealed semesters will be opened once, after the method, the parameters and the
primary metric have been frozen, and their numbers reported separately. That
evaluation is not completed here. The second judging platform's sealed id block and
the corroborating trace's sealed year stay shut, and the frozen pipeline covers the
main trace alone (Section~\ref{sec:sealed}).

\paragraph{The budget shape is selected on one set of cells, and does not always
transfer.}
The promise holds for any budget under the cap, so the shape decides only how much of
the base policy's benefit survives, and we select it on \devnum{15} multiserver
validation cells that do not include $k = 1$. Supplementary Section~S3.14 gives the
single-server configuration where that choice does not transfer, what the pre-stated
rule reports there, and why an operator with a much smaller pool should re-run the
selection instead of taking our shape.

\paragraph{What the recorded costs mean.}
The cost we replay is the platform's recorded execution time, a wall-clock duration for
one evaluation that already contains contention we then re-impose inside the pool.
Supplementary Section~S2.1 gives how much of it is contention, and why the observed
\devnum{60}-second regime is not documentation of an enforced timeout. It also records
that the released ACcoding tables \cite{accoding2024} carry no submission time, although
the platform recorded one, so its arrival
process is synthetic, and Supplementary Section~S2.3 reports which quantile of the
Firefox CI refit does not survive out of sample.

\paragraph{History availability and the recorded-queue counterexample.}
Original-clock scores may use outcomes that are still pending in the replay. The exact
protocol certifies that its final replay uses none, cell by cell, and it is the
headline for that reason. It does not certify an online fixed point, in which a
deployed predictor is driven by completion events as they happen, and it does not
certify a deployment in which copied users share state across copies. The conservative
reference is not a clean control for availability: it also reduces history and refits
models, and about a quarter of its fall from the original figure comes from the refit
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}).

LPC-EGEE \cite{lpc_egee_trace} provides both recorded waits and concentrated costs, but its fitted
first-come first-served approximation underestimates the chronological-test mean waits,
and the one reading on which a guard fires is a per-class pool we impose ourselves. We
therefore treat its policy replays as conditional counterfactuals rather than validated
estimates of deployment benefit, and Supplementary Sections~S6.4 and~S6.6 give both
readings with the six objections the per-class one does not answer.

\paragraph{Not studied.}
Preemption is outside the model, so nothing here applies to a system that can
checkpoint or suspend. The wrapper applies unchanged to a learned or
reinforcement-learned base policy, since its bound assumes nothing about the base, but
we did not test one. Capacity is fixed within each replay. The capacity sweep is a
sensitivity to that choice rather than an estimate of the original platform's
capacity, and it is not a capacity-planning study. The reported runs compare policies
at a common set of enqueue instants and do not charge the cost of computing features
and running the predictor. Section~\ref{sec:prediction} measures that cost and charges
it in a sensitivity, where it moves the closed gap by less than \devnum{0.001} at
every load. What is missing is therefore not the latency but the rest of an end-to-end
system. The local timed-work service was executed, but there was no prospective
production deployment and no measurement of user feedback. ALPS \cite{fu2024alps},
a prototype serverless scheduler of the kind this wrapper is meant for, has no proved
per-job bound.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

An operator can now answer the student whose submission sat behind the looping one.
The queue is reordered by predicted cost, and no submission waits more than $G$ seconds
longer than it would have in arrival order, whatever the predictor does. We find that
work, not position, is the unit the excess is written in. A job's delay relative
to first-come first-served equals its net overtaken work divided by the number of
servers, up to a constant multiple of the time limit, and a budget on that quantity is
sufficient for a per-job guarantee and, up to an additive $O(kL)$, necessary. A
maximum waiting time makes a promise of the same form, and at the busiest load and tight promises it keeps
much less of the benefit, because it charges a job for the backlog ahead of it as well as
for the jobs that pass it. Counting overtakes is not necessary,
and it gives a guarantee only at the worst-case charge of the time limit per pass. On
the main trace that charge is \devnum{150} times the mean job cost. With scores that use only results available in the replay,
% Source: prechecks/cost_ratio/out_mean_cost_ratio.txt (L / mean cost = 150.1, mean 0.3997 s).
Guard($\devnum{600}$) closes $\devnum{0.672}$ of the reference gap at the busiest
load, against $\devnum{0.848}$ for unguarded ordering. These are development results,
conditional on the constructed demand and on the information protocol. Four of the
original six workloads have tail waits too short relative to $L$ for the uniform
certificate to be useful. Concentration of job costs is an empirical opportunity for
reordering, and we do not claim it is necessary.

% Guard(600)|exact and SPJF-E|exact, gap_closed.

We have not run the guard in production. The timed-work service reproduces the dispatch
rule but not the ideal simulator's waits, and the sealed terms are still sealed. The
graph model's edges add a small rank-correlation gain over all test rows,
\devnum{0.006}, and a larger one, \devnum{0.028}, on tasks with fewer than five prior
results, \devnum{1{,}092} of the \devnum{40{,}844} test rows here.
% Source: evidence/predictor_neural/out_evaluate_core.txt:1-2 (test rows 40,844;
% cold-start exercises 1092).
Two questions are left open. The identity is stated in the dispatch-sequence convention,
where the constant is exactly determined in both directions. Under the executed-work
convention the upward constant is unchanged, but we know only that the downward one
lies in $[(k-1)L, 2(k-1)L]$; closing that gap would make the two conventions
interchangeable. And Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} is not claimed to be the least
restrictive rule carrying its guarantee: whether some wrapper overrides the base policy
strictly less often and still promises $G$ is a question we have not settled in either
direction.
