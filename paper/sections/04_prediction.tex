\section{Cost Prediction}
\label{sec:prediction}

Before the looping submission of the introduction starts, something has to decide that
it will be long, from history alone and at the instant it arrives. That decision is a number, which the scheduler of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} reads. We say here what
the number estimates, how its inputs stay causally visible, which model families we compared under one
protocol, and which model enters the queue. All figures are development figures, from the traces of Section~\ref{sec:data}.

\subsection{Two Targets}

A scheduler and an evaluation want different things from the same model. The scheduler
wants the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[C_i \mid x_i]$, which is the single-server
pairwise-interchange target and the candidate score fitted in Section~\ref{sec:rank}.
In addition, that section explains why the interchange argument does not establish optimality
on several servers, where we evaluate the order empirically. For comparing predictors we
also need a target that stays interpretable when the mix of jobs shifts between
periods, so we score each model on a binary label. A job is \emph{heavy} when its
executed cost $C_i$ exceeds the 95th percentile of the training rows. This one definition of heavy serves the whole paper, including the worst heavy-job wait
of Section~\ref{sec:model}. On the main judging trace
the cut-off is \devnum{1.559} s and heavy jobs are \devnum{1.31\%} of the test period, and on the second judging trace they are \devnum{1{,}314} ms and
\devnum{5.07\%}. The label is
fixed on training rows and never refit on the period being scored. On the main trace its
rate in the test period is about a quarter of its training rate, \devnum{1.31\%} against
\devnum{5.00\%}, while on the second it stays at about the training rate. Because the positive class is small
we report average precision next to AUROC. For the regression output we report RMSE on
$\log(1+C)$ and Spearman correlation, since a ranking policy consumes order rather than
level.

\subsection{The Visibility Protocol, Made Operational}

Section~\ref{sec:model} defines visibility by result availability, so information about job $j$ may enter $x_i$ exactly when $\mathrm{done}_j + \delta \le a_i$. Turning that
into a feature pipeline takes three commitments.

First, every historical feature is recomputed by streaming the log in availability
order and reading each entity's state as of the query instant. No feature is assembled
over the whole period and masked afterwards. We test this by recomputing the features
from scratch and comparing, and \devnum{0} rows disagree in any of the eight
configurations. Supplementary Section~S1 gives the audit in full, including the same
test applied to the natural but wrong construction and the separate checks on the
graph pipeline.

Second, thresholds are refit per target period. We recompute the heavy cut-off and the
class boundaries used inside the models from the training rows of that target, and we
predict each target period with a model fitted only on periods before it and frozen
before the target's first arrival. Training rows whose results would not have been
available by that instant are dropped, and on the primary configuration the rule binds on
\devnum{0} rows. We report the zero because a rule that never binds is easily mistaken for an absent rule.

Third, we treat the delay $\delta$ with which results reach the feature store as a
sensitivity, not as an assumption. Supplementary Section~S1 gives the two clocks in
full. Under them, ranking survives a ten-minute feedback delay while the probability
calibration gets worse.

All families see the same rows, the same split and the same availability rule.
Supplementary Section~S1 defines the three families and their ablation ladders. The
gradient-boosted tabular groups M1 to M5 add task history, user history, static
features of the submitted artefact and one-hop relational aggregates in turn. The
sequence family R1 and R1S is a GRU over the user's own earlier jobs, used alone and as
a state vector inside the boosted model. The relational ladder N0, G0U, G0, G1 and G2 is
a heterogeneous network that starts from a multilayer perceptron on M4's columns
and adds empty neighbour slots, then the real edges, then edges permuted inside a
period and time block. Two entries are controls rather than models. M6 scrambles each
job's neighbourhood, and G2 permutes the edges. If the relational structure carries
signal, these two must lose it.

\subsection{What the Comparison Shows}

Supplementary Table~S2 gives the two judging traces. One-hop relational aggregates add nothing on either, since M5 $-$ M4 is \devnum{$+0.0019$} of AUROC on the first and
\devnum{$-0.0002$} on the second. Similarly, the ladder gives the same result with a trained model
in place of hand-built aggregates. Deleting every edge (G0) and permuting the edges
(G2) leave AUROC within \devnum{0.002} of G1, and the paired intervals for G1 $-$ G0
(\devnum{$-0.0001$ $[-0.0019, 0.0018]$}) and G1 $-$ G2 (\devnum{$+0.0019$ $[-0.0000,
0.0042]$}) cover zero. The one step of the ladder that resolves is N0 $\rightarrow$
G0U, \devnum{$+0.0139$ $[+0.0090, +0.0195]$} of AUROC at identical information. What
moves there is the encoder, with the edge set held empty in both.

Edges do carry something, confined to cold-start tasks. On the
\devnum{1{,}092} test rows whose task has fewer than five visible prior results, G1
beats M4 by \devnum{$+0.1464$ $[+0.0997, +0.1887]$} of Spearman correlation, and beats
its own permuted-edge control by \devnum{$+0.0282$ $[+0.0130, +0.0432]$}. Over all rows
the G1 $-$ G2 Spearman difference is \devnum{$+0.0063$ $[+0.0014, +0.0111]$}, small and resolved, while the corresponding AUROC and AUPRC differences are not. Thus we
read this as a task property rather than a verdict on relational models. When each entity's own recent
record is visible and rich, a neighbourhood adds little except for an entity with no record yet.

The sequence state has the best prediction scores of anything we fitted, and no
consistent effect in the queue. R1S reaches AUROC \devnum{0.9366} against M4's
\devnum{0.9283}, a difference of \devnum{$+0.0083$ $[+0.0038, +0.0131]$}, and it also
wins on RMSE and Spearman. Inside the queue we find no consistent improvement. Against
the same ranking objective it gains \devnum{$+0.007$ $[0.003, 0.011]$} of the
deadline-window gap at busy-hour utilisation \devnum{0.8}, loses
\devnum{$-0.010$ $[-0.022, -0.002]$} at \devnum{0.5}, and is open at
\devnum{1.0}. A control fitted on the same \devnum{90\%} of rows that the GRU's early
stopping left available moves the same way, so the smaller training set does not
explain it.

Which information matters is not a constant across platforms. The user's own history leads on the first judging trace, and the task's history leads on the second by \devnum{0.11} of AUROC (Supplementary Table~S2). The four non-judging traces spread wider still, and Supplementary Section~S1.2 reads them by feature group, the AUROC column of Table~\ref{tab:cross} carrying the figure the scheduling results are read against.

\subsection{Cost of Predicting, and What Enters the Scheduler}

Prediction has to be cheap enough to sit in front of a queue whose median job is
\devnum{204} ms. It is. Charging the measured per-job cost of each family to the
predictive policies before they enqueue changes the closed gap by less than
\devnum{0.001} at every load we ran (Supplementary Section~S1).

Which model enters the queue is a queueing decision, not a prediction decision. M5
has the higher AUROC of the two tabular candidates and loses in the queue at all three
loads, by \devnum{0.012}, \devnum{0.008} and \devnum{0.010} of the deadline-window
gap, with intervals excluding zero in each case. R1S has the higher AUROC of
everything we fitted and does not win consistently. Therefore we take M4, the full tabular model, into Section~\ref{sec:scheduling}. We selected it on the queueing
outcome on validation periods, not on prediction quality. Thus the best predictor was not the best scheduler, and the two orderings do not agree.
