\section{Scheduling Algorithm}
\label{sec:scheduling}

The scheduler has two layers. A base policy orders the queue by a score computed at
arrival, and a guard limits how much work may overtake any waiting job. The guard accepts
any base policy, and its guarantee does not depend on that policy behaving well.

\subsection{Ranking by Expected Cost}
\label{sec:rank}

When a server frees, the base policy starts the waiting job with the smallest score. For a
batch on one server, the pairwise-interchange argument orders jobs by their conditional
mean costs. However, this conclusion does not carry over to $k \ge 2$ servers. On two
servers, let job $u$ cost $2$ and job $w$ cost $3$, and let job $v$ cost $0.1$ with
probability $0.9$ and $100$ with probability $0.1$. Ordering by conditional mean starts $u$
and $w$ and gives an expected mean wait of $2/3$, whereas starting $u$ and $v$ gives
$0.096667$. We therefore treat expected-cost ordering as an empirically motivated base
policy, not as a claim of optimality.

Squared error on raw seconds and the Tweedie deviance both have the conditional mean as
their population optimum. Their fitted orders can nevertheless differ with finite model
capacity, link function and regularisation, so we compare the objectives empirically, with
the features and model of Section~\ref{sec:prediction} held fixed. We fit the expected-cost
policy, shortest predicted job first on the expected-cost score (SPJF-E), on raw seconds
with a Tweedie loss of power $1.5$, which accommodates mass near zero and a long right tail
\cite{jorgensen1987edm,yang2018tweedieboost}. For the log-scale policy, shortest predicted
job first on the log-scale score (SPJF-log), we fit the same pipeline by squared error on
$\log(1+C)$. By Jensen's inequality, the back-transform of this fit is no larger than the
conditional mean.

We use only the order of a score. Its level does not transfer between periods, because the
share of heavy jobs differs between training and test periods by a factor of several, so
the scheduler never reads a score as a time or a threshold. Section~\ref{sec:exp_rank}
compares these and other objectives in the queue. No choice of score bounds the worst case.
Whatever the score, a job it underestimates can be passed again and again, and only the
guard below bounds that.

\subsection{The Overtake-Budget Guard}
\label{sec:guard}

Fix a base policy $A$. For every waiting job $q$ and every time $t$ let
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{over}[q](t) \;=\;
  \sum \{\, C_j \;:\; j > q,\ j \text{ has completed by } t \,\},
  \label{eq:over}
\end{equation}
the total cost of the jobs that arrived after $q$ and have finished by $t$. Such a job ran
while $q$ waited, so it overtook $q$. Each waiting job has a budget $\mathrm{budget}(q,t)$
on this quantity, and the job is \emph{eligible} when its account has reached its budget,
$\mathrm{over}[q](t) \ge \mathrm{budget}(q,t)$. Whenever a server is free and the queue is
not empty, the guard starts the earliest-arrived eligible job if there is one, and
otherwise lets $A$ choose (Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} and Figure~\ref{fig:guard}). Serving
the earliest-arrived eligible job matters when budgets differ between jobs. It ensures that
whenever a job $j$ starts ahead of an earlier-arrived waiting job $i$, the job $i$ was not
eligible at that instant, and the per-job bound of Section~\ref{sec:theory} rests on this
step. With a constant budget $B$ the account is non-increasing in arrival order, so the
rule reduces to a test on the head of the queue, and $B = 0$ gives FCFS.

The guarantee reads the budget only through an upper bound on it. If
$0 \le \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \le B_{\max}$ for every waiting job at every instant, then every
job waits at most $W_{\mathrm{FCFS}} + B_{\max}/k + (3-2/k)L$, whatever the base policy,
the arrivals and the predictions (Theorem~\ref{thm:guard}). Below the cap, the budget decides
how much of the base policy's benefit survives and which jobs pay for it. We study the
family
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \;=\;
  \min\bigl(\,B_0 + \gamma\,n_q + \eta\,k\,(t - a_q),\ B_{\max}\,\bigr),
  \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
with $B_0, \gamma \ge 0$, $0 \le \eta < 1$ and $n_q$ the number of jobs waiting when $q$
arrives. We study three shapes in this family, namely \emph{constant} ($\gamma = \eta = 0$),
\emph{age-relative} ($\eta > 0$) and \emph{queue-length} ($\gamma > 0$). In the
age-relative shape, $\eta$ is the share of the system's capacity, over a job's own wait,
that may go to jobs that arrived after it, so the allowance grows with the wait the job has
already had. The term $\gamma n_q$ widens the
allowance only for jobs that arrive into a long queue. All of $B_0$, $\gamma$ and
$B_{\max}$ are measured in work and scale with $k$.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Overtake-budget guard wrapped around a base policy $A$}
\label{alg:guard}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{parameters:} cap $B_{\max} \ge 0$; shape $B_0, \gamma \ge 0$, $\eta \in [0,1)$; base policy $A$
\State \textbf{state:} waiting set $Q \gets \emptyset$; $\mathrm{over}[q] \gets 0$ for every job $q$
\While{some job has not started}
  \State $t \gets$ next event time
  \ForAll{jobs $c$ completing at $t$} \Comment{$C_c$ becomes known here and nowhere earlier}
    \ForAll{$q \in Q$ that arrived before $c$}
      \State $\mathrm{over}[q] \gets \mathrm{over}[q] + C_c$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \ForAll{jobs $i$ arriving at $t$}
    \State $n_i \gets |Q|$; \ $Q \gets Q \cup \{i\}$; \ $\mathrm{over}[i] \gets 0$
  \EndFor
  \While{a server is free \textbf{and} $Q \neq \emptyset$} \Comment{dispatch decision}
    \State $E \gets \{\, q \in Q : \mathrm{over}[q] \ge \min(B_0 + \gamma n_q + \eta k (t - a_q),\, B_{\max}) \,\}$
    \If{$E \neq \emptyset$}
      \State $j \gets$ the earliest-arrived member of $E$ \Comment{guard fires}
    \Else
      \State $j \gets A(Q, t)$ \Comment{base policy decides}
    \EndIf
    \State start $j$ on the free server; \ $W[j] \gets t - a_j$; \ $Q \gets Q \setminus \{j\}$
  \EndWhile
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Setting the Guard}

An operator states a bound $G$, the number of seconds by which any job may exceed its FCFS
wait, and sets $B_{\max} = k\bigl(G - (3 - 2/k)L\bigr)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:params}). No
data are needed for this step. A bound of at most $(3-2/k)L$ can be guaranteed this way only
with $B_{\max} = 0$, which is FCFS. The shape parameters $B_0$, $\gamma$ and $\eta$ do not
affect the bound, and we choose them on validation data by a rule fixed in advance
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_prov}). At $G = \devnum{600}$ s this rule selects the age-relative
shape with $B_0 = \devnum{30}k$ s of work and $\eta = \devnum{0.75}$ (Supplementary
Table~S5).

The guard charges the executed cost of an overtaker when it completes, because a job's cost
is known only then. A job still running contributes nothing yet. When the last overtaker of
a job $i$ starts, up to $k-1$ earlier overtakers may still be running, and the one starting
is not charged either. The work that actually passes $i$ can therefore exceed the budget by
at most $kL$. This is what the additive term of the guarantee pays for, and it is why
the guard needs no prediction to enforce its bound. With a Fenwick tree over completed work
and a segment tree over the waiting jobs, each event costs $O(\log n)$, and all budget
arithmetic is done in integer microseconds, so the eligibility test is exact
(Supplementary Section~S5).
