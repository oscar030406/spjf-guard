\section{Scheduling Algorithm}
\label{sec:scheduling}

The scheduler has two layers. A base policy orders the queue by a score computed at
arrival, and a wrapper limits how much work may overtake any waiting job. The two are
independent, in that the wrapper accepts any base policy and its guarantee does not
depend on that policy behaving well.

\subsection{Ranking by Expected Cost}
\label{sec:rank}

When a server frees, the base policy takes the waiting job with the smallest score.
Since service is non-preemptive, a dispatched job holds its server for its entire
service time, and the delay it imposes on every job still queued is $C_i$ itself
rather than a monotone transform of $C_i$. Waiting times add in the units in which
service is measured, so the score should estimate the conditional mean
$\mathbb{E}[C_i \mid x_i]$.

This is not what a log-scale regression produces. Fitting squared error on
$\log(1+C)$ and inverting gives a conditional geometric mean, which differs from the
conditional mean by a term that grows with the conditional variance, and in a
heavy-tailed cost distribution that term sits on the small fraction of jobs the
scheduler most needs to push back. We change the training objective and nothing else,
keeping the features, the model size and the rolling-origin protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} as they are, with the target taken in raw seconds under a
Tweedie loss of power $1.5$, which accommodates a distribution with most of its mass
near zero and a long right tail \cite{jorgensen1987edm,yang2018tweedieboost}. We write
SPJF-E for the policy that ranks the queue by this estimate of expected cost, and
SPJF-log for the same pipeline fitted on $\log(1+C)$ and inverted.

Only the order of the fitted score is used. Its level does not transfer across
periods, since the share of heavy jobs in the training periods differs from the target
period by a factor of several, so we never read the score as a quantity, neither as an
estimated finish time nor as a threshold. The scheduler is therefore invariant to a
monotone recalibration of the score.

Section~\ref{sec:exp_rank} measures what the change of estimand is worth, together with
the alternatives we tried and rejected: a predicted quantile, a two-class rule, and
squared error on raw seconds. It also measures the asymmetry of the errors, which is
what makes the estimand matter: treating a heavy job as light and putting it in front
costs about an order of magnitude more than the reverse mistake, so a score fitted for
symmetric accuracy optimises the wrong thing.

No choice of ranking function bounds the worst case. A job the score misjudges can still
be passed indefinitely, whatever the score is and however it was fitted;
Section~\ref{sec:guard} is what bounds it.

\subsection{The Overtake-Budget Guard}
\label{sec:guard}

Fix a base policy $A$. For every waiting job $q$ and every time $t$ define
\[
  \mathrm{over}[q](t) \;=\;
  \sum \{\, C_j \;:\; j > q \text{ in rank, } j \text{ has completed by } t \,\},
\]
the total true service of the higher-ranked jobs that have finished by $t$. Such a job
arrived at or after $a_q$, so it necessarily ran while $q$ waited. Job $q$ is allowed a
budget $\mathrm{budget}(q,t)$ on that quantity, and the fired set at a
dispatch epoch is
$E(t) = \{\, q \text{ waiting} : \mathrm{over}[q](t) \ge \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \,\}$.
At every dispatch epoch, that is, at every instant at which a server is free and the
queue is non-empty, the wrapper serves the minimum-rank member of $E(t)$ when $E(t)$
is non-empty and otherwise serves whatever $A$ chooses (Figure~\ref{fig:guard}).

Serving the minimum-rank member matters when budgets differ between jobs. It
guarantees that whenever a job $j$ is dispatched ahead of a lower-ranked waiting job
$i$, the job $i$ was not in $E(t)$, hence $\mathrm{over}[i](t) < \mathrm{budget}(i,t)$
at that very instant. The per-job bound of Section~\ref{sec:theory} rests on that
step. With a constant budget $\mathrm{budget}(q,t) \equiv B$ the quantity
$\mathrm{over}[q](t)$ is non-increasing in rank, so $E(t)$ is non-empty exactly when
the queue head belongs to it and the rule degenerates to a test on the head. Setting
$B = 0$ recovers FCFS.

\paragraph{The Cap Carries the Promise; the Shape Does Not.}
The guarantee of Section~\ref{sec:theory} reads the budget only through an upper
bound on it: if $0 \le \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \le B_{\max}$ for every waiting job and
every instant, then every job waits at most
$W_{\mathrm{FCFS}} + B_{\max}/k + (3-2/k)L$, for any base policy, any arrival
sequence and any predictions. Everything below the cap is a design freedom that
changes how much of the base policy's benefit survives and which jobs pay for it,
and changes the promise not at all. We study the one-line family
\[
  \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \;=\;
  \min\bigl(\,B_0 + \gamma\,n_q + \eta\,k\,(t - a_q),\ B_{\max}\,\bigr),
\]
with $B_0, \gamma \ge 0$, $0 \le \eta < 1$ and $n_q$ the number of jobs waiting when
$q$ arrives, and read three shapes off it: \emph{constant} ($\gamma = \eta = 0$),
\emph{age-relative} ($\eta > 0$), and \emph{queue-length} ($\gamma > 0$). The
coefficient $\eta$ says that a waiting job tolerates at most a fraction $\eta$ of the
system's entire service capacity, over its own waiting time, being spent on jobs that
arrived after it, so the allowance grows with the wait a job has already had; being a
fraction of capacity it is dimensionless and needs no retuning when $k$ or the load
changes. The term $\gamma n_q$ instead widens the allowance only for jobs that arrive
into a queue that is already long, and is flat in the wait. $B_0$, $\gamma$ and
$B_{\max}$ are measured in work and scale with $k$.

Only the age-relative shape carries a second, multiplicative bound,
$W < (W_{\mathrm{FCFS}} + B_0/k + (3-2/k)L)/(1-\eta)$, and only when $\gamma = 0$:
with $\gamma > 0$ the constant part of the budget differs from job to job, so the
multiplicative form holds per job with that job's own constant, and no single
number is available for the operator to state in advance
(Remark~\ref{rem:mult_scope}). For
$\gamma > 0$ we claim the additive bound alone.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Overtake-budget guard wrapped around a base policy $A$}
\label{alg:guard}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{parameters:} cap $B_{\max} \ge 0$; shape $B_0, \gamma \ge 0$, $\eta \in [0,1)$; base policy $A$
\State \textbf{state:} waiting set $Q \gets \emptyset$; $\mathrm{over}[q] \gets 0$ for every job $q$
\While{some job has not started}
  \State $t \gets$ next event time
  \ForAll{jobs $c$ completing at $t$} \Comment{$C_c$ becomes known here and nowhere earlier}
    \ForAll{$q \in Q$ with $q < c$ in rank}
      \State $\mathrm{over}[q] \gets \mathrm{over}[q] + C_c$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \ForAll{jobs $i$ arriving at $t$}
    \State $n_i \gets |Q|$; \ $Q \gets Q \cup \{i\}$; \ $\mathrm{over}[i] \gets 0$ \Comment{$n_i$ fixes $i$'s constant part}
  \EndFor
  \While{a server is free \textbf{and} $Q \neq \emptyset$} \Comment{dispatch epoch}
    \State $E \gets \{\, q \in Q : \mathrm{over}[q] \ge \min(B_0 + \gamma n_q + \eta k (t - a_q),\, B_{\max}) \,\}$
    \If{$E \neq \emptyset$}
      \State $j \gets$ the minimum-rank member of $E$ \Comment{guard fires}
    \Else
      \State $j \gets A(Q, t)$ \Comment{base policy decides}
    \EndIf
    \State dispatch $j$ on the free server; \ $W[j] \gets t - a_j$; \ $Q \gets Q \setminus \{j\}$
  \EndWhile
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Parameters from a service promise.}
The operator states $G$, the number of seconds by which any job may exceed its FCFS
waiting time, and reads off $B_{\max} = k\bigl(G - (3 - 2/k)\,L\bigr)$, the inversion
of the bound proved in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. No data are needed for this step. The rule requires $G > (3 - 2/k)L$; below that threshold no
positive budget is admissible, which is not an artefact of the wrapper but a floor of
order $L$ that Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows to be unavoidable for any wrapper of
this family that overtakes at all, if its promise is to hold whatever base policy it
is given. The promise $G$ follows from $B_{\max}$ alone.
The shape parameters $B_0$, $\gamma$ and $\eta$ decide how much of the base policy's
benefit survives and which jobs pay for it, so we choose them on a validation trace
that excludes the test period; that pool is held out of the selection and not of the
development process, and Section~\ref{sec:exp_prov} states what the selection therefore
does and does not establish. The rule is fixed before the grids are run: three
pre-stated grids, one per shape, \devnum{258} points in all; a point is feasible when
its harm is at most $G/2$ in every one of the \devnum{15} validation cells; the
objective is the deadline-window gap closed in the worst cell, with ties going to
smaller worst harm and then to smaller $B_0$, $\eta$, $\gamma$. Guard($G$) is the
winner over the three grids together; the winner inside each grid is reported as an
ablation row. Table~\ref{tab:setup} gives the grids and what they selected.

\paragraph{Cost per event.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} is written for readability rather than for execution, since
its inner loop over waiting jobs at each completion is linear. We keep
$\mathrm{over}[q] = TC - F(q)$ instead, where $TC$ is the total completed work and
$F(q)$ the prefix sum over ranks up to $q$ of completed work, maintained in a Fenwick
tree, so that a completion is one point update and a query one prefix sum. The firing
test is a segment tree over the window of waiting ranks supporting suffix range-add
and the query ``leftmost leaf with value at least $T$'', after rewriting
$\mathrm{over}[q](t) \ge B_{0,q} + \eta k (t - a_q)$, where $B_{0,q} = B_0 + \gamma n_q$
is fixed at $q$'s arrival, with $\eta k = e_n/e_d$ in lowest terms as
$e_n a_q - e_d B_{0,q} - e_d F(q) \ge e_n t - e_d\, TC$,
whose left side is a per-job value changed only by range-add at completions and whose
right side depends on $t$ alone. The capped channel needs only the head, since at a
fixed threshold $\mathrm{over}$ is non-increasing in rank. All budget arithmetic is
carried in integer microseconds, so the test is exact and no floating-point clock
enters the schedule. Each event costs $O(\log n)$.

\paragraph{What is charged, and why no service time is needed at dispatch.}
The budget is charged the true executed work of overtakers, and only when they
complete. At the instant the guard decides, it has read $C_j$ only for jobs that have
already finished, and a job still in service contributes nothing. That is what makes
the wrapper implementable in a system that learns a job's cost at its completion, and
it is also why the bound carries an additive term in place of an exact
accounting. When the last overtaker of $i$ is dispatched, up to $k-1$ further
overtakers may be in service with their work not yet charged, and the overtaker being
dispatched is not charged either, so the work that actually passes $i$ can exceed the
budget by at most $kL$. Under the timeout at $L$, executed work means $\min(C_j, L)$;
the log records that quantity and the guard charges it, so no assumption on raw
sizes enters.

\paragraph{Why the budget is measured in work and not in positions.}
Earlier bounded-overtaking mechanisms limit how many times a job may be passed, which
is the currency of the finite-skip and Nudge-$K$
rules~\cite{grosof2021nudge,vanhoudt2022nudgek,grosof2022wcfs}. Counting is not
necessary: a job can be passed any number of times and stay inside its promise, provided
the work that passes it is small. Counting is sufficient in this model, but only through
the worst-case charge of $L$ per overtake, since every overtaker is bounded by the time
limit; a count of $\kappa$ therefore buys a promise of $(\kappa + 2k-2)L/k$
(Remark~\ref{rem:counts}, \eqref{eq:skip}). That is the bound a count can give, and it
is loose by the ratio $L/\bar{C}$, one to two orders of magnitude on our traces. Ten
overtakers that each run for the full limit $L$ and ten that run for ten milliseconds
carry the same count and delays that differ by three orders of magnitude, so the charge
is paid on every dispatch whether or not the work is there.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} runs the count rule as a baseline and measures how much
the looseness costs on a real load. That work is the currency the excess is actually
written in is the content of the identity of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, not a claim of
ours about what earlier mechanisms could not do.
