\subsection{The Headline: Scores That Use Only Available Results}
\label{sec:exp_visibility}

The prediction study of Section~\ref{sec:prediction} releases a job's result at the time the
original platform recorded it. In the simulated queue, a job completes at a time that
depends on the policy, so a score computed on the original clock may use a result that the
simulated queue has not produced yet. The \emph{exact} protocol removes this. It freezes the
fitted model, simulates a policy, and withholds every result that was not yet complete in
that simulation when the job using it arrived. It then recomputes the affected histories
and scores and simulates again, until no score uses an unavailable result. The set of
withheld results only grows, so the procedure stops, and in every development cell it ends
with no unavailable result in use. Three references stand beside the exact protocol. The
\emph{original} protocol keeps the platform's clock and is optimistic. The
\emph{conservative} protocol admits a result only an hour after it appeared, uses only
history from the same class-term copy, and refits the models. The \emph{static} protocol
uses no outcome history at all. The guard's parameters are the same under all four.

At the busiest load, Guard($\devnum{600}$) under the exact protocol reaches a
$\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ of \devnum{118.94} s and closes \devnum{0.672 [0.602, 0.743]} of
the gap (Table~\ref{tab:visibility}). The same guard closes \devnum{0.801 [0.764, 0.842]}
under the original protocol, \devnum{0.429 [0.357, 0.533]} under the conservative one and
\devnum{0.349 [0.281, 0.457]} under the static one. Unguarded SPJF-E closes \devnum{0.848}
under the exact protocol. The conservative figure falls further than the exact one because
it also withholds history that the queue would have had and refits the models.
Supplementary Section~S3.7 separates the two effects. The parameters were selected under
the original protocol, and we do not know in which direction a selection under the exact
protocol would move the figure. The full grids were run under the original protocol, so the
rest of this section uses it unless stated otherwise, and its figures are optimistic
references.

\subsection{What to Rank By}
\label{sec:exp_rank}

Ranking by expected cost beats the log-scale score at the two higher loads and ties with it
at the lightest. The paired difference in gap closed is
\devnum{$-0.000$ $[-0.016, 0.013]$}, \devnum{$+0.012$ $[0.004, 0.019]$} and
\devnum{$+0.019$ $[0.011, 0.024]$} at $\rho = \devnum{0.5}$, \devnum{0.8} and \devnum{1.0}.
Refitting the log-scale model from scratch moves this difference by at most \devnum{0.001}
(Supplementary Table~S16). Quantile scores and squared error on raw seconds lose to the
Tweedie fit at every load. A rule that only separates predicted-heavy from predicted-light
jobs recovers little of the benefit, so most of the benefit comes from the order inside the
light class (Supplementary Table~S14).

The two kinds of prediction error do not cost the same. Replacing the largest
\devnum{1\%} of underestimates by the true cost closes \devnum{0.91} of the distance to SJF
in $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ at $\rho = \devnum{1.0}$ on both overlays tested. Repairing
the largest \devnum{1\%} of overestimates closes only \devnum{0.26} and \devnum{0.24}. At
\devnum{0.1\%}, the repair of underestimates closes \devnum{0.47} and that of overestimates
\devnum{0.04}. Treating a long job as short is thus the expensive mistake, by about an
order of magnitude. The repair depends on the score's numerical scale, so this result
describes our fitted predictor and not prediction errors in general.

\subsection{What the Guarantee Costs and Gains}
\label{sec:exp_guard}

Table~\ref{tab:guard} gives the guard at three bounds and three loads. At $\rho =
\devnum{1.0}$, Guard($\devnum{600}$) closes \devnum{0.801} of the gap and the unguarded
policy \devnum{0.916}. A bound of ten time limits therefore costs \devnum{0.115} of the gap.
The cost is \devnum{0.087} at $G = \devnum{1200}$ s and \devnum{0.438} at $G =
\devnum{300}$ s. The paired intervals of these three differences are
\devnum{[-0.142, -0.077]}, \devnum{[-0.098, -0.064]} and \devnum{[-0.526, -0.314]}, and none
contains zero, so the guarantee has a cost at every $G$ we tested. What the guarantee buys
is protection of the worst job. The unguarded policy delays one job at the busiest load by
\devnum{5{,}904.5} s beyond its FCFS wait, \devnum{98.4} times the \devnum{60}-second limit.
The guard keeps every job within its bound. It also keeps the worst wait among heavy jobs
within about \devnum{450} s of its FCFS value, whereas the unguarded policy multiplies that
wait by \devnum{2.8} to \devnum{4.1}.

The harm limit used in selection does not always carry over to the evaluation overlays.
Guard($\devnum{600}$) has harms of \devnum{319.6} and \devnum{339.9} s at the two higher
loads, above the \devnum{300} s validation limit, although every excess stays within the
bound. The shape of the budget matters mainly where the bound is tight and the load high.
There it decides which jobs are delayed more than how much of the benefit survives
(Supplementary Sections~S3.3 and~S3.11). Counting overtakes instead of work gains almost
nothing on this load (Supplementary Figure~S2). Overtakers here are mostly shorter than a
second, while a count must be sized against the worst case of $L$ per pass. Our count
baseline is charged when an overtaker starts. Charging it at completion instead changes its
gap closed by at most \devnum{0.015}, and by at most \devnum{0.001} at the two higher loads.
An aging baseline ranks by $\hat C_i - \omega(t - a_i)$, with the credit $\omega$ chosen
under the same harm limit. At the busiest load under the conservative protocol it closes
\devnum{0.051} of the gap, against \devnum{0.429} for the guard, and one job exceeds its
FCFS wait by \devnum{855.6} s. Aging carries no proved bound (Supplementary Table~S12).

Ordering by predicted cost inside fixed windows, as in the closest scheduler for judging
queues \cite{zhang2024ojscheduling}, limits overtaking by position. We ran a version of it
on the first overlay with windows of up to \devnum{512} jobs. It closes at most
\devnum{0.417}, \devnum{0.146} and \devnum{0.057} of the gap at the three loads, with
largest excesses of \devnum{232.7}, \devnum{359.6} and \devnum{444.5} s. On the same overlay

\devnum{189.3}, \devnum{199.7} and \devnum{219.6} s. Near saturation, a window large enough

deliberately, by reversing them, by drawing them at random, or by calling the longest

protects the worst single job but not the shape of the tail, and a guarded run may have a
worse percentile than FCFS, by at most $G$ (Supplementary Table~S8).

\subsection{The Bounds on the Traces}
\label{sec:exp_bound}
\label{sec:exp_k1}

Every run that includes a bounded policy checks the bound on every job inside the
simulation, so a violation would expose an error in the code rather than in the theorem.
The \devnum{315} policy-by-cell runs reported here make \devnum{5{,}554{,}949{,}400} such
checks. The single-server runs add \devnum{67{,}396{,}287}, and an independently written
second simulator adds \devnum{5.7} billion. None of them finds a violation, and the count of
violations is \devnum{0}. Proposition~\ref{prop:clock} was checked job by job on the same
overlays and Proposition~\ref{prop:absolute} on all \devnum{304} bounded runs, also with
\devnum{0} violations. Proposition~\ref{prop:late} was checked on constructed instances
(Supplementary Section~S4.12). The ratio of the realised worst excess to the bound falls as
$k$ grows, from \devnum{0.9950} at $k = 1$ to \devnum{0.8850} at $k = 8$. At one server
Guard($\devnum{600}$) reaches \devnum{595.5} s of its \devnum{600} s bound (Supplementary
Section~S3.6). The residual $\Delta_i$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} never exceeds
\devnum{0.518} of its own bound $2(k-1)L$ (Supplementary Sections~S3.13 and~S3.16).
% Source: outputs/dev_tables/bound_checks.csv (315 rows, jobs_checked summing to
%   5,554,949,400, violations 0, used_over_allowed maxima 0.9311 at k = 4 ... 0.8850 at
%   k = 8); outputs/dev_tables/k1/bound_checks.csv (21 rows, 67,396,287 checks, worst
%   0.9950); outputs/dev_tables/identity_residuals.csv (max ratio 0.5172712).

The absolute bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:absolute} comes close to the realised waits. At
one server every FCFS wait equals its fluid backlog to the microsecond, as the proposition
predicts, and the largest wait reaches \devnum{0.999} of the bound. At the three loads the
worst wait under a work budget reaches \devnum{0.886}, \devnum{0.951} and \devnum{0.963} of
the bound, and under FCFS \devnum{0.881}, \devnum{0.945} and \devnum{0.965}. An absolute
bound quoted in advance needs a forecast of the burst $\sigma$. The burst of the validation
terms predicts that of the evaluation terms only to within a factor of \devnum{0.738} to
\devnum{1.573}, and it is too low in \devnum{10} of \devnum{15} cells. A bound quoted from
it is broken in \devnum{18} of \devnum{60} runs, by up to \devnum{349.8} s. Such a bound
therefore needs a margin, here a factor of \devnum{1.36} on $\sigma$, or an admission
control that keeps the arrivals within the quoted burst.

\subsection{Where the Method Applies}
\label{sec:exp_cross}

Table~\ref{tab:cross} places the main log beside the other workloads. Excesses are in units
of each log's time limit $L$, the unit in which the guarantee is written. Two screens say
where the guard is useful. The first compares the FCFS tail with the guarantee's additive
term $(3-2/k)L$, which is close to $3L$. If the FCFS p99 is below this term, the guarantee
is coarser than the waits themselves. CodeBench, at \devnum{4.56}$L$, and the cloud-function
log, at \devnum{4.22}$L$, clear the first screen, and four of the six workloads do not. The
compute farm is the extreme case, with an FCFS p99 of \devnum{18.7} h against $L =
\devnum{493{,}857}$ s, or \devnum{5.7} days. The second screen measures how concentrated the
costs are, which is the opportunity for reordering. In the two CI pools the longest
\devnum{1\%} of jobs carry only \devnum{3.4\%} and \devnum{5.2\%} of the work. There,
ordering by true cost makes the p99 worse in three of the four windows we examined. Where
both screens are cleared, the guard closes \devnum{0.48} to \devnum{0.71} of the gap and
holds the largest excess to \devnum{2.5} to \devnum{3.8}$L$ at a bound of \devnum{5}$L$.
Clearing both screens does not guarantee a gain, as the next subsection shows.

\subsection{Capacity, Physical Runs and Feedback}
\label{sec:exp_physical}
\label{sec:exp_sens}

The benefit depends on capacity. We ran every $k$ from \devnum{1} to \devnum{20} on the five
overlays. At $k = \devnum{1}$, Guard($\devnum{600}$) makes $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ worse
by \devnum{493.79} s, and all five overlays agree that it improves the p99 only for $k =
\devnum{4}$ to \devnum{12}. We also ran the guard physically, on two worker processes that
occupy real time slots for each job's recorded duration. This run executed
\devnum{2{,}151} jobs of one development window, and all \devnum{6{,}453} dispatch decisions
followed the rule. The guarded p99 of \devnum{1{,}783.65} s was nevertheless no better than
FCFS's \devnum{1{,}763.30} s, and single jobs differed from the ideal simulation by up to
\devnum{1{,}354.10} s. A second physical test used the two-worker back end of a course
platform, where twenty scripted accounts submitted \devnum{100} jobs per cell in a
deadline-shaped burst with $G = \devnum{400}$ s. Unguarded predicted order left \devnum{7}
jobs beyond the bound. The four guarded cells left none, with a worst excess of
\devnum{0.514}$G$ (Supplementary Sections~S6.1, S6.2 and~S6.9).
% Sources: prechecks/weakness1_attack/summary_numbers.json and capacity_curve.csv;
% prechecks/weakness1_attack/physical_numbers.json and physical_verification.json;
% prechecks/platform_testbed/records/summary.json (jobsOverPromise, ratio 0.5138).

The simulations replay arrivals at their recorded times, so they neither reward a scheduler
with the work it would attract nor punish it for the users it would drive away
\cite{feitelson2021resampling,shmueli2009simulation}. The logs record one part of that
feedback, the time between one result and the same student's next submission. In a second
replay, each submission is released only after its predecessor's result plus that recorded
delay. This replay shortens $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ by a factor of \devnum{1.3} to
\devnum{3.8}. Guard($\devnum{600}$) then closes \devnum{0.825}, \devnum{0.809} and
\devnum{0.810} of the gap at the three loads, against \devnum{0.734}, \devnum{0.827} and
\devnum{0.800} with the recorded arrival times, and it keeps every job within its bound
(Supplementary Section~S6.5).
