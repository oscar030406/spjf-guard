\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Ordering by the predicted number closes most of the gap to true shortest-job-first, and
it leaves one job almost a hundred job-limits behind. What it costs to remove that job
is what this section measures. The predictors are taken as given from
Section~\ref{sec:prediction}. We ask what a scheduler should sort by
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_rank}), what the per-job guarantee costs and what it gains
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}), whether the bounds of Section~\ref{sec:theory} hold on
real traces (Section~\ref{sec:exp_bound}), and where the method applies
(Sections~\ref{sec:exp_cross} and~\ref{sec:exp_sens}).

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

The main scheduling comparisons replay the overlaid CodeBench pool built in
Section~\ref{sec:overlay}. The three load levels differ only in the pool size and the
five overlays only in the per-copy week shift, and every policy sees the same arrival
instants and the same service times, so the comparison is pathwise.
Supplementary Table~S4 collects the configuration and the selection protocol; the
metrics are defined in Section~\ref{sec:model} and the policy names in
Table~\ref{tab:policies}, extended by the aging comparator below.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility} gives the headline under the exact protocol, in which
every score uses only results available in the replay. The original-score ranking,
budget-shape, corrupted-score, residual and single-server results retain the source
result clock and are optimistic references, not estimates under the exact protocol. The capacity and physical experiments use an archived
development fit and are reported separately. We report the fraction of the FCFS-to-SJF gap closed and the
plain percentage reduction against FCFS side by side throughout, because they are
different numbers and the first is easy to read as the second.


\subsection{What Was Selected on What}
\label{sec:exp_prov}

Three statements precede the numbers. The guard family and a coarse grid over
$(B_0, \eta)$ were explored on the evaluation trace before any validation-only selection
existed, so the selection chooses inside a family already narrowed on the trace this
section reports. Note that nothing here is held out. The validation pool is the
development terms minus the last one. That last term is held out of the selection, not
of the development process, and it cannot load a pool on its own
(Section~\ref{sec:overlay}). We therefore describe no figure in this section as out of
sample, as validated on test data, or as evidence of generalisation. The grids, the
feasibility rule, the objective and the tie-break
were fixed before the grids were run, at comparable density across the three shapes, with
exact integer microseconds for arrivals, service and budget arithmetic. Supplementary
Section~S5 records what the selection chose, which results the released package
reproduces and which remain archived development evidence, and the drop-one-cell
sensitivity of the worst-cell rule. No sealed result is printed here.

\subsection{Outcome Visibility in the Replayed Queue}
\label{sec:exp_visibility}

Does the score a replayed job is ranked by use only results that would have been
available in the replay? The original feature sweep releases an outcome at its
source-platform $\mathrm{done}_j$, whereas under policy $P$ the replayed job completes
at $a_j+W_P[j]+C_j$. A feature may therefore be causal in the recorded log and still
use a result that is not available in the replay, and neither a future-record
perturbation test nor a brute-force audit of the source-clock features resolves that.
The exact protocol resolves it by construction. It freezes the fitted model, replays
a policy, leaves out every same-copy result that the replay says was not complete when
the job using it arrived, recomputes the affected histories and scores, and replays
again, until no used result is unavailable. The set of left-out results only grows, so
the procedure terminates, and every development cell ends with no used result
unavailable. Two
references sit beside it. The conservative score restricts user, exercise and pair
history to submission outcomes in the same class-term copy, admits a result only when
$\mathrm{done}_j+\devnum{3600}\le a_i$, and refits its models under those
restrictions, so it leaves out more than the replay requires. The static score uses
code, course and clock information known at submission, without outcome history.
Guard parameters remain those selected under the original score in every protocol. A
lag audit of the conservative reference finds \devnum{0} violations in every
conservative Guard and FCFS cell, and \devnum{336} job occurrences beyond the lag
under unguarded conservative SPJF-E. Supplementary Section~S3.7 gives the audit, the
same-copy exposure decomposition, the aging comparator outside the budget family and
the package predictor metrics with their splits.

At the busiest load, Guard($\devnum{600}$) under the exact protocol gives p99
\devnum{118.94} s and closes \devnum{0.672 [0.602, 0.743]} of the reference gap,
against \devnum{89.24} s and \devnum{0.801 [0.764, 0.842]} under the original score
and \devnum{174.71} s and \devnum{0.429 [0.357, 0.533]} under the conservative score
(Table~\ref{tab:visibility}). Unguarded SPJF-E closes \devnum{0.848} under the exact
protocol and \devnum{0.670} under the conservative one. The static control closes
\devnum{0.349 [0.281, 0.457]}. The conservative figure can be taken apart: applying
its three restrictions to the frozen original model gives \devnum{0.536}, and
refitting the models under them accounts for the rest of the fall to
\devnum{0.429}, so most of that fall comes from the refit and not from the results
left out. Leaving out, in addition, the results of other classes in the same term moves the
exact figure to \devnum{0.655}. These intervals condition on the constructed
development overlays and do not cover semester selection, model refitting or
deployment variation.

\subsection{What to Rank By}
\label{sec:exp_rank}

Supplementary Table~S16 gives the unguarded policies at the three loads. Ranking by expected
cost is ahead of the log-scale score at the two higher loads and open at the lightest:
the paired difference in gap closed is
\devnum{$-0.000$ $[-0.016, 0.013]$} at $\rho = \devnum{0.5}$,
\devnum{$+0.012$ $[0.004, 0.019]$} at $\rho = \devnum{0.8}$ and \devnum{$+0.019$ $[0.011,
0.024]$} at $\rho = \devnum{1.0}$. The control for refit noise, the same log-scale estimand
refitted from scratch, moves the gap by \devnum{$+0.001$ $[-0.002, 0.002]$},
\devnum{$-0.000$ $[-0.001, 0.001]$} and \devnum{$-0.000$ $[-0.001, 0.000]$}, so the advantage
is larger than the noise of refitting and is not produced by it. That control and the
sequence state come from the earlier development study and are the two rows of
Supplementary Table~S16 the package does not produce. The sequence state of
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} does not translate into a consistent gain, and we do not
claim it as an improvement.


Alternative ranking losses fitted on the same features and run through the same
simulator are Supplementary Table~S14: the fitted quantile scores and the raw-second
squared-error model lose to the Tweedie fit at every load, and a two-class rule that
only separates predicted-heavy from predicted-light jobs recovers little of the
benefit, so most of it comes from the fine ordering inside the light class.

The two kinds of error do not cost the same. We find that the damage is concentrated
in a few underestimates. Replacing the largest \devnum{1\%} of underestimates by the
true cost, and leaving the rest of the score alone, closes \devnum{0.91} of the
distance to the SJF reference in the deadline-window p99 at $\rho = \devnum{1.0}$, on
both overlays. Repairing the largest \devnum{1\%} of overestimates instead closes only
\devnum{0.26} and \devnum{0.24}. At \devnum{0.1\%} the two figures are \devnum{0.47}
and \devnum{0.04} (Supplementary Section~S3.16 gives the six waits behind them).
Treating a heavy job as light and letting it run first is the expensive mistake, by
about an order of magnitude. This identifies the fitted predictor: replacing a score by
the true cost depends on its numerical scale and is not invariant to a monotone score
transformation. It neither establishes a universal error asymmetry nor distinguishes
the population targets of raw squared error and Tweedie loss.

The jobs that pay are the heavy ones, and the worst wait among them is the last of the
secondary metrics of Section~\ref{sec:model}. The unguarded ranking's worst excess of
\devnum{5{,}904.5} s at the busiest load is the hundred job-limits of the introduction:
\devnum{98.4} times the \devnum{60}-second limit, for one job. The guard leaves the
worst heavy job inside the promise at every load, within about \devnum{450} s of the
FCFS figure, where the unguarded ranking multiplies it by between \devnum{2.8} and
\devnum{4.1}; Supplementary Section~S3.16 gives the nine waits.


\paragraph{What the shape of the budget decides.}
The promise is the cap's alone, so the three shapes are compared at a fixed promise,
and the question is only how much of the base policy's benefit each keeps and who
pays for it. The shape matters where the promise is tight and the load is high, and
hardly at all elsewhere. It decides which jobs are delayed more than it
decides how much of the benefit survives. Supplementary Section~S3 gives the paired
differences at the three promises, the harm axis on which the queue-length shape
comes within \devnum{0.009} of the joint winner's gap at under half its worst harm,
and the equal-promise Fixed($G$) comparator that the harm rule rejects at every
promise while leading on the gap in eight of the nine cells.

Counting positions gains almost nothing on this load (Supplementary Figure~S2,
dotted). This measures how loose the count bound is here, not whether a count can give
a bound at all. The conversion of Supplementary Section~S4.4 does give one, and the
count rule is run at the largest
count that bound allows at each promise. What makes it loose is the cost distribution.
Overtakers on this trace are overwhelmingly shorter than a second, while a count of
$\kappa$ has to be sized against the worst case $\kappa L$. The count is therefore
exhausted long before the work it stands for is, and the baseline forces most
dispatches back to the queue head. It is charged at dispatch; Supplementary
Section~S3.16 gives the passes that allows and what charging at completion instead
would change.

Corrupted scores separate what the guard does from what it does not do
(Supplementary Table~S8, Supplementary Figure~S3). Under a reversed order, a random order, or a
score that calls the truly longest \devnum{1\%} the shortest, the unguarded policy delays some
job by more than \devnum{6{,}000} s beyond FCFS at full load, and Guard($\devnum{600}$) holds
every job inside its promise. The deadline-window p99 is not rescued: it stays at
\devnum{546.5}, \devnum{261.9} and \devnum{543.6} s against \devnum{273.50} s for FCFS, which is
worse than FCFS for two of the three. The guarantee is per job and additive, so this is what it
permits: a guarded run may have a worse quantile than FCFS, by at most $G$, and what it
removes is the worst single job and not the shape of the tail.



\subsection{The Bounds on the Traces}
\label{sec:exp_bound}

Every bound in Section~\ref{sec:theory} is asserted on every job of every run, inside
the simulation rather than afterwards on summary statistics, so a violation would be a
failure of the code and not of the theorem. That is \devnum{5{,}554{,}949{,}400} per-job
checks over the \devnum{315} policy-by-cell runs reported here, \devnum{67{,}396{,}287}
more in the single-server configuration and \devnum{5.7} billion in an independently
written second simulator, with \devnum{0} violations anywhere. The ratio of the realised
worst excess to the bound falls as $k$ grows, from \devnum{0.9950} at $k = 1$ to
\devnum{0.8850} at $k = 8$, while the finite-skip bound is attained exactly on small
instances. The residual of Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} never exceeds \devnum{0.518} of
its own ceiling $2(k-1)L$, and Table~\ref{tab:resid} reports it on four cells.
Supplementary Sections~S3.13 and~S3.16 give the identity measured job by job, the
cross-check against the second simulator, the ratio at each $k$, and why a search that
does not build the cascade of Theorem~\ref{thm:tight1} stops where it does.

\subsection{A Single Server}
\label{sec:exp_k1}

At $k = 1$ the additive constant $(3 - 2/k)L$ is exactly $L$, the identity of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} holds with no error term, and the separation between budget shapes is
largest (Supplementary Table~S10). Guard($\devnum{600}$) and Fixed($\devnum{600}$) are
statistically indistinguishable on the gap closed. They differ by a factor of
\devnum{3.41} in harm, and the same comparison gives \devnum{3.01} at
$G = \devnum{300}$ s and \devnum{12.18} at $G = \devnum{1200}$ s, so the looser the
promise, the more the shape of the budget matters. The realised excess reaches
\devnum{595.5} s against the promised \devnum{600} s, and under the worst corrupted
score \devnum{597.0} s. At one server almost all of the promise is used.

The single-server configuration also shows the cost of choosing a shape on one set of
cells: the parameters were selected on the \devnum{15} multiserver validation cells,
and $k = 1$ is not among them, so the joint winner transfers at the two tighter promises
and not at $G = \devnum{1200}$ s (Supplementary Section~S3.14). We report the number the
pre-stated rule produces and do not re-tune it on this cell;
Section~\ref{sec:limits} states what it means.

Two empirical screens say where reordering and the uniform certificate may be useful.
The first compares the FCFS tail scale with the certificate's additive term
$(3-2/k)L$. Four of the original six workloads have smaller FCFS p99 values. That
means the certificate is too coarse at that scale, not that finer promises are
impossible for a restricted base policy or workload. CodeBench at \devnum{4.56}$L$ and
the serverless trace at \devnum{4.22}$L$ clear it. The compute farm is the extreme,
with an FCFS p99 of \devnum{18.7} h against a bound $L=\devnum{493{,}857}$ s, or
\devnum{5.7} days, and guards that never fire. The second screen is cost
concentration, an opportunity for reordering rather than a necessary condition. In the
CI pools the longest \devnum{1\%} carry only \devnum{3.4\%} and \devnum{5.2\%} of
work, and true-size ordering makes p99 worse on three of the four reported windows. Where
both screens are cleared, in the original-score CodeBench and serverless comparisons,
the guard closes \devnum{0.48} to \devnum{0.71} of the reference gap and limits
observed excess to \devnum{2.5} to \devnum{3.8}$L$ at a promise of \devnum{5}$L$.
Clearing either screen does not guarantee a percentile improvement: the capacity
experiment below contains counterexamples.

Which screen a trace clears can depend on how its pool is drawn. Read as one pool per
walltime class rather than one per site, LPC-EGEE's \devnum{900}-second class has an
FCFS p99 of \devnum{13.76}$L$. It is the one reading of that log on which the
work-budget guard fires, spending \devnum{0.993} of its allowance against
\devnum{0.046} pooled. Cost concentration inside it falls to \devnum{0.213} from the
\devnum{0.407} of the pooled trace. The row's footnote and Supplementary Section~S6.6
say why that is not evidence of deployment benefit.

\subsection{Capacity and Physical Implementation}
\label{sec:exp_physical}

Benefit changes sign with capacity. Across every integer $k=\devnum{1},\ldots,\devnum{20}$
on the five development overlays, Guard(\devnum{600}) makes deadline p99 worse by
\devnum{493.79} s at $k=\devnum{1}$, and positive simultaneous support for its
improvement occurs only at $k=\devnum{4}$--\devnum{12}. The selected load points are
therefore not evidence of positive benefit at every capacity. The physical timed-work
experiment executes \devnum{2{,}151} jobs on two workers in a selected empty-start
release window: all \devnum{6{,}453} observed-prefix decisions pass the rule audit, but
the guard's per-job difference from its ideal measured-input replay reaches
\devnum{1{,}354.10} s. Its physical p99 is \devnum{1{,}783.65} s against
\devnum{1{,}763.30} s for separately timed FCFS, so this window does not establish a
guarded p99 gain. Supplementary Sections~S6.1 and~S6.2 give the sweep protocol, the
selection rule and the positive execution checks beside these negative results.

\subsection{Sensitivities}
\label{sec:exp_sens}


Five trace-construction variants of an earlier source-clock pipeline are a sensitivity
to that pipeline and not to the selected Guard($G$); Supplementary Table~S15 and
Supplementary Section~S3.17 give them, and Supplementary Section~S5 gives the
configuration and the release boundary. A sixth variant changes the arrival process
instead of the trace construction. The
logs carry a recorded think time, the offset between one result and that student's
next submission. Releasing each submission after its predecessor's result plus that
offset shortens every wait by a factor of \devnum{1.3} to \devnum{3.8}. The baseline
gains most, because its own longer waits delay its own users more. The absolute gap
therefore narrows while the fraction of it a policy closes does not: Guard(\devnum{600})
closes \devnum{0.825}, \devnum{0.809} and \devnum{0.810} of it, against
\devnum{0.734}, \devnum{0.827} and \devnum{0.800} at the recorded instants. The cost
of the promise falls with the queue, from \devnum{41.6\%} above unguarded SPJF-E to
\devnum{3.7\%}, and the guarantee holds on all \devnum{17{,}634{,}760} jobs of
each run against first-come first-served on that run's own realised arrivals. These
runs read the original-clock scores of the main development cells, so they are a
sensitivity to the arrival process and not a repeat of the exact-protocol headline.
Supplementary Section~S6.5 gives the table, the bootstrap and the limits, the first
being that the recorded think times were produced on a platform where no student
waited.
