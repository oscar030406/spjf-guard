\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments are organised by the three questions of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Question~1,
whether the cost of a job can be estimated before it runs, is answered in
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} and is not repeated here. This section takes the predictors as
given and asks what a scheduler should sort by (Section~\ref{sec:exp_rank}), what the per-job
guarantee costs and what it buys (Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}), whether the bounds of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} hold on real traces (Section~\ref{sec:exp_bound}), and where the
method applies (Sections~\ref{sec:exp_cross} and~\ref{sec:exp_sens}).

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

The main scheduling comparisons replay the overlaid CodeBench pool built in
Section~\ref{sec:overlay}. The three load levels differ only in the pool size and the
five overlays only in the per-copy week shift, and every policy sees the same arrival
instants and the same service times, so the comparison is pathwise.
Supplementary Table~S4 collects the configuration and the selection protocol; the
metrics are defined in Section~\ref{sec:model} and the policy names in
Table~\ref{tab:policies}, extended by the aging comparator below.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility} gives the conservative-visibility headline. The
original-score ranking, budget-shape, corrupted-score, residual and single-server
results retain the source result clock and are optimistic references, not estimates
of the conservative protocol. The capacity and physical experiments use an archived
development fit and are reported separately. We report the fraction of the FCFS-to-SJF gap closed and the
plain percentage reduction against FCFS side by side throughout, because they are
different numbers and the first is easy to read as the second.


\subsection{What Was Selected on What}
\label{sec:exp_prov}

Three statements have to precede the numbers. First, the guard family and a coarse grid over
$(B_0, \eta)$ were explored on the evaluation trace before any validation-only selection
existed, so the selection reported above chooses inside a family that had already been narrowed
on the trace this section reports. Second, nothing here is held out. The validation pool is the
development terms minus the last one, which is held out of the selection and not of the
development process; that term cannot load a pool on its own
(Section~\ref{sec:overlay}) and is therefore not evaluated separately. We do not describe any
figure in this section as out-of-sample, validated on test
data, or evidence of generalisation.


Third, the grids, the feasibility rule, the objective and the tie-break were fixed
before the grids were run, at comparable density across the three shapes, and the
package comparisons use exact integer microseconds for arrivals, service and budget
arithmetic. Supplementary Section~S5 records what the selection chose, which results
the released package reproduces and which remain archived development evidence, and
the drop-one-cell sensitivity of the worst-cell rule. No sealed result is printed
here.

\subsection{Outcome Visibility in the Replayed Queue}
\label{sec:exp_visibility}

The original feature sweep releases an outcome at its source-platform
$\mathrm{done}_j$. Under policy $P$, the corresponding replayed job completes at
$a_j+W_P[j]+C_j$. A feature may therefore be causal in the recorded log and still
use a result unavailable in the replay. Neither a future-record perturbation test
nor a source-clock brute-force feature audit resolves this discrepancy.

The conservative score restricts user, exercise and pair history to submission
outcomes in the same class-term copy, and admits a result only when
$\mathrm{done}_j+\devnum{3600}\le a_i$; the static score uses code, course and clock
information known at submission, without outcome history. Both use their own fitted
models, so the three variants differ in more than release delay and their differences
cannot be attributed to queueing delay alone. Guard parameters remain those selected
under the original score, and the conservative variant is the empirical headline.
Supplementary Section~S3 gives the same-copy exposure decomposition, the lag audit
that finds \devnum{0} violations in every conservative Guard and FCFS cell and
\devnum{336} job occurrences beyond the lag under unguarded conservative SPJF-E, the
pending policy-specific exact variant, the aging comparator outside the budget family
and the package predictor metrics with their splits.




At the busiest load, conservative Guard($\devnum{600}$) gives p99
\devnum{174.71} s and closes \devnum{0.429 [0.357, 0.533]} of the reference gap,
against \devnum{89.24} s and \devnum{0.801 [0.764, 0.842]} under the original
score. The static control closes \devnum{0.349 [0.281, 0.457]}; the paired
conservative-minus-static gap is \devnum{0.080 [0.039, 0.124]}. Thus retained
history improves on the static control here, but the original-score effect is
substantially larger. These intervals condition on the constructed development
overlays and do not cover semester selection, model refitting or deployment variation.

Alternative ranking losses fitted on the same features and run through the same
simulator are Supplementary Table~S13: the fitted quantile scores and the raw-second
squared-error model lose to the Tweedie fit at every load, and a two-class rule that
only separates predicted-heavy from predicted-light jobs recovers little of the
benefit, so most of it comes from the fine ordering inside the light class.

The two kinds of error do not cost the same. Replacing the largest \devnum{1\%} of
underestimates by the true cost, and leaving the rest of the score alone, moves the
deadline-window p99 at $\rho = \devnum{1.0}$ from \devnum{66.57} s to \devnum{45.01} s on the
first overlay and from \devnum{65.02} s to \devnum{45.32} s on the second, which is
\devnum{0.91} of the distance to the SJF reference in both. Repairing the largest
\devnum{1\%} of overestimates instead moves it to \devnum{60.37} s and \devnum{59.72} s, which
is \devnum{0.26} and \devnum{0.24} of that distance. At \devnum{0.1\%} the two figures are
\devnum{0.47} and \devnum{0.04}. Treating a heavy job as light and letting it run first is the
expensive mistake, by about an order of magnitude. This diagnoses the fitted predictor: replacing a score by true cost depends on its
numerical scale and is not invariant to a monotone score transformation. It neither
establishes a universal error asymmetry nor distinguishes the population targets of
raw squared error and Tweedie loss.

\subsection{Question 3: What the Guarantee Costs and Buys}
\label{sec:exp_guard}

Table~\ref{tab:guard} reports the guarded policies at three promises and three loads, and
Supplementary Figure~S2 plots the gap closed against the promise. The cost of the promise
depends on the promise and on the load. Under the original source-clock score, an
optimistic reference rather than the headline of Table~\ref{tab:visibility}, at
$\rho = \devnum{1.0}$ Guard($\devnum{600}$) closes \devnum{0.801} of the FCFS-to-SJF
gap against \devnum{0.916} for the unguarded policy, so it
gives up \devnum{0.115} of the gap at a promise of ten job-limits, about an eighth of what the
unguarded ordering buys; at $G = \devnum{1200}$ s the cost is \devnum{0.087} and at
$G = \devnum{300}$ s, five job-limits, \devnum{0.438}. The paired week-block intervals for
these three differences are \devnum{[-0.142, -0.077]}, \devnum{[-0.098, -0.064]} and
\devnum{[-0.526, -0.314]}; none contains zero, so the promise has a resolved cost at every
$G$ we tested.

\paragraph{What the shape of the budget is worth.}
The promise is the cap's alone, so the three shapes are compared at a fixed promise
and the question is only how much of the base policy's benefit each keeps and who
pays for it. The shape matters where the promise is tight and the load is high, and
hardly at all elsewhere; it decides which jobs pay for the promise more than it
decides how much of the benefit survives. Supplementary Section~S3 gives the paired
differences at the three promises, the harm axis on which the queue-length shape
comes within \devnum{0.009} of the joint winner's gap at under half its worst harm,
and the equal-promise Fixed($G$) comparator that the harm rule rejects at every
promise while leading on the gap in eight of the nine cells.

Counting positions buys almost nothing on this load
(Supplementary Figure~S2, dotted). This measures how loose the count bound is here, not
whether a count can give a bound at all: \eqref{eq:skip} does give one, and the count rule is
run at the largest count that bound allows at each promise. What makes it loose is the cost
distribution. Overtakers on this trace are overwhelmingly shorter than a second, while a count
of $\kappa$ has to be sized against the worst case $\kappa L$, so the count is exhausted long
before the work it stands for is, and the baseline
forces most dispatches back to the queue head. It is charged at dispatch, which allows
\devnum{34} rather than \devnum{30} passes at
$k = \devnum{4}$ and $G = \devnum{600}$ s; charging at completion instead changes the gap closed
by at most \devnum{0.015}, and by at most \devnum{0.001} at the two higher loads.

Corrupted scores separate what the guard does from what it does not do
(Supplementary Table~S7, Supplementary Figure~S3). Under a reversed order, a random order, or a
score that calls the truly longest \devnum{1\%} the shortest, the unguarded policy delays some
job by more than \devnum{6{,}000} s beyond FCFS at full load, and Guard($\devnum{600}$) holds
every job inside its promise. The deadline-window p99 is not rescued: it stays at
\devnum{546.5}, \devnum{261.9} and \devnum{543.6} s against \devnum{273.50} s for FCFS, which is
worse than FCFS for two of the three. The guarantee is per job and additive, so this is what it
permits: a guarded run may have a worse quantile than FCFS, by at most $G$, and what it
removes is the worst single job and not the shape of the tail.



\subsection{The Bounds on the Traces}
\label{sec:exp_bound}

Every guarded run asserts the per-job bound of Section~\ref{sec:theory} for each job
separately, inside the simulation rather than afterwards on summary statistics. Across the
\devnum{315} policy-by-cell runs reported here, that is \devnum{5{,}554{,}949{,}400} per-job
checks with \devnum{0} violations, and the single-server configuration adds \devnum{21} runs and
\devnum{67{,}396{,}287} checks, also with \devnum{0} violations. An independently written
simulator, which shares no code with ours, reproduces the runs job for job on
\devnum{17{,}634{,}760} jobs and \devnum{22} policies with \devnum{0} differing jobs, and asserts
the same bounds on its own waiting times over \devnum{5.7} billion checks with \devnum{0}
violations; that cross-check was run against the earlier development pipeline, whose dispatch
sequence the package reproduces.

The ratio of the realised worst excess to the bound falls as $k$ grows. It reaches
\devnum{0.9950} at $k = 1$, \devnum{0.9311} at $k = 4$, \devnum{0.9219} at $k = 5$,
\devnum{0.8975} at $k = 7$ and \devnum{0.8850} at $k = 8$. These are facts about the traces and
leave the constant where Section~\ref{sec:theory} put it: $(3-2/k)L$ is the exact supremum at every $k$,
but the family that approaches it is a geometric cascade that needs hundreds of jobs arranged
against the wrapper, and nothing in a replayed workload arranges them. The
finite-skip bound, by contrast, is attained exactly on small instances, at a ratio of
\devnum{1.0000}.


\paragraph{The identity, measured job by job.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} is asserted on every job of every cell of overlay~0:
\devnum{0} violations over \devnum{1{,}428{,}415{,}560} job-checks. The residual
$D_i = k(W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i]) - (\mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i)$ stays far
from its ceiling: over all \devnum{81} cells the largest is \devnum{0.5173} of the
bound $2(k-1)L$, so it never exceeds \devnum{0.518}, which is about $(k-1)L$. That is
where a search that does not build the cascade of Theorem~\ref{thm:tight1} stops, so
the measurement says the workload does not attain the constant, not that the constant
is loose. Table~\ref{tab:resid} reports four cells and Supplementary Table~S8 all
nine; Supplementary Section~S3 gives the recording kernels and their validation, the
same-phase shares, and the variance of the excess that
$(\mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i)/k$ explains.

\subsection{A Single Server}
\label{sec:exp_k1}

At $k = 1$ the additive constant $(3 - 2/k)L$ is exactly $L$, the identity of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} holds with no error term, and the separation between budget shapes is
largest (Supplementary Table~S9). Guard($\devnum{600}$) and Fixed($\devnum{600}$) are
statistically indistinguishable on the gap closed and differ by a factor of \devnum{3.41} in
harm; the corresponding factors are \devnum{3.01} at $G = \devnum{300}$ s and \devnum{12.18} at
$G = \devnum{1200}$ s. The realised excess reaches \devnum{595.5} s against the promised
\devnum{600} s, and under the worst corrupted score \devnum{597.0} s, so at one server the
promise is close to being spent.

The single-server configuration also shows the cost of choosing a shape on one set of
cells: the parameters were selected on the \devnum{15} multi-server validation cells,
and $k = 1$ is not among them. Supplementary Section~S3 gives the five gaps that show
the joint winner transferring at the two tighter promises and not at
$G = \devnum{1200}$ s. We report the number the pre-stated rule produces and do not
re-tune it on this cell; Section~\ref{sec:limits} states what it means.

Two empirical screens assess where reordering and the uniform certificate may be
useful. The first compares the FCFS tail scale with the certificate's additive term
$(3-2/k)L$. Four of the original six workloads have smaller FCFS p99 values; this
means that this certificate is too coarse at that scale, not that finer promises
are impossible for a restricted base policy or workload. CodeBench at
\devnum{4.56}$L$ and the serverless trace at \devnum{4.22}$L$ clear this screen.
The compute farm is the extreme: the FCFS p99 is \devnum{18.7} h against a bound
$L=\devnum{493{,}857}$ s, or \devnum{5.7} days, and the tested guards never fire.
The second screen is cost concentration, an opportunity for reordering rather than
a necessary condition. In the CI pools the longest \devnum{1\%} carry only
\devnum{3.4\%} and \devnum{5.2\%} of work, and true-size ordering worsens p99 on
three of the four reported windows. In the original-score CodeBench and serverless
comparisons, the guard closes \devnum{0.48} to \devnum{0.71} of the reference gap
and limits observed excess to \devnum{2.5} to \devnum{3.8}$L$ at a promise of
\devnum{5}$L$. Clearing either screen does not guarantee a percentile improvement:
the capacity experiment below contains counterexamples.

\subsection{Capacity and Physical Implementation}
\label{sec:exp_physical}

The supplementary capacity sweep tests the archived score at every integer
$k=\devnum{1},\ldots,\devnum{20}$ on all five development overlays and extends
the first overlay to its exact zero-wait endpoint $k=\devnum{76}$. Benefit changes
sign with capacity: at $k=\devnum{1}$, Guard(\devnum{600}) worsens deadline p99
by \devnum{493.79} s, while positive simultaneous support for its improvement
occurs only at $k=\devnum{4}$--\devnum{12}. The selected load points are therefore
not evidence of positive benefit at every capacity. The sweep preserves complete
trace state, uses an archived fit and leaves the offered work fixed.

The physical timed-work experiment executes \devnum{2{,}151} jobs on two workers
in a selected empty-start release window. All \devnum{6{,}453} observed-prefix
decisions pass the rule audit, but the guard's per-job discrepancy from its ideal
measured-input replay reaches \devnum{1{,}354.10} s. Its physical p99 is
\devnum{1{,}783.65} s against \devnum{1{,}763.30} s for separately timed FCFS,
so this window does not establish a guarded p99 gain. Supplementary Section~S6
reports the positive execution checks beside these negative results, explains
the stall-corrected observed-trace certificate and distinguishes shadow FCFS on
the guard's measured input from the separately executed baseline.

\subsection{Sensitivities}
\label{sec:exp_sens}


Five trace-construction variants were run through an earlier source-clock pipeline:
they are sensitivity results for its stored M4 log-scale score and fixed
completed-work budget, not for the selected expected-cost Guard($G$), and within that
earlier configuration the alternatives retain a positive gap while the earlier
fixed-budget guard ranges from \devnum{0.442} to \devnum{0.843} of the gap at full
load. They support sensitivity of the earlier ordering experiment to timestamp
interpretation, source-term inclusion and co-ending-run treatment, and do not validate
robustness of the final selected Guard($G$), conservative history or policy-specific
exact visibility. Supplementary Table~S14 gives the five variants; Supplementary
Section~S5 gives the configuration and release boundary explicitly.

Two further limits of these runs --- what the intervals do and do not cover, and the
prediction cost the runs do not charge --- are stated in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
