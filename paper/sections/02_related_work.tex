\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Scheduling with predictions}

Purohit, Svitkina and Kumar \cite{purohit2018predictions} frame predictions in online algorithms as a trade-off between consistency and robustness, and Mitzenmacher \cite{mitzenmacher2020misprediction} analyses a non-preemptive priority queue whose classes come from predicted sizes; our argument for ranking by expected cost is a sample-path counterpart of the latter. Scully, Grosof and Mitzenmacher \cite{scully2022uniform} assume, in a preemptive M/G/1, that the estimate of a job of size $s$ lies in $[\beta s, \alpha s]$, show that feeding those estimates to SRPT can leave the approximation ratio unbounded, and give SRPT with Bounce, which is within $3.5\,\alpha/\beta$ of SRPT in expected response time, alongside PSJF with Estimates, which is within $\alpha/\beta$ of PSJF and so within $1.5\,\alpha/\beta$ of SRPT. Worst-case analyses give ratios for total flow time that degrade with the distortion of the predictions instead of requiring it in advance \cite{azar2021flowtime,azar2022distortion}; others cover noisy estimates in a many-server limit \cite{dong2025sjf}, the cost of obtaining a prediction \cite{shahout2024skippredict}, and the maximum stretch over all jobs on one preemptive machine when sizes are predicted \cite{zhao2025fairpredictions}. The survey of Mitzenmacher and Shahout \cite{mitzenmacher2025queueing} leaves open what a system should do when a predictor degrades. Our guarantee differs on four axes: the reference, which is first-come first-served and not an optimal or SRPT schedule; the scope, which is a single job and not a mean; the assumptions, which are an arbitrary arrival sequence and arbitrary predictions and not Poisson arrivals with bounded multiplicative error; and the service model, which is non-preemptive. A bound relative to first-come first-served is weaker than one relative to an optimum, since first-come first-served is itself poor under heavy-tailed costs; it keeps a job from a catastrophic wait and claims no improvement by itself.

\subsection{Fairness relative to FCFS, and bounded overtaking}

Comparing a job against the schedule it would have received under first-come first-served is established in parallel job scheduling. Sabin, Kochhar and Sadayappan \cite{sabin2004fairness} use a job's fair start time, its start time in a re-simulation with the later arrivals removed, in a \emph{strict} variant that still lets the job backfill and a \emph{relaxed} one in which it may not start until every earlier-queued job has started; Sabin and Sadayappan \cite{sabin2005unfairness} carry both into a family of unfairness metrics, the excess of the actual start over the fair start time. Our reference is the relaxed variant, and on identical servers it does not matter whether the later arrivals are removed from the counterfactual, because first-come first-served dispatches in arrival order and they never move an earlier job's start. Rajbhandary, Bunde and Leung \cite{rajbhandary2013fairness} use both variants as job-level metrics for conservative-backfilling policies. In all of this the quantity is computed after the fact. The exception is PV-EASY \cite{yuan2010pveasy,yuan2014strictfairness}, which enforces \emph{strict fairness}, defined there as no job being delayed by any job of lower priority, online and with runtime predictions, by preempting the backfilled jobs that stand in the way of the highest-priority waiting job. It reaches a zero excess with kill-and-restart preemption, which Section~\ref{sec:limits} places outside our model, and it proves nothing about the schedule it produces. Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} says why the trade is needed: a wrapper of our family that overtakes at all cannot promise less than $L$ if the promise is to hold around every base policy, so a bounded guarantee rather than a zero one is the only thing available to us. We are not aware of a scheduler that bounds the fair-start-time excess online \emph{without} preemption, or of an upper bound proved for it. Backfilling gives guarantees of a different shape, a reservation against each job's own arrival-time schedule in the conservative variant and one for the queue head in the aggressive variant \cite{lifka1995easy,mualem2001backfilling,lindsay2013backfilling}: per job and enforced online, but not a comparison against a reference policy.

Bounded overtaking has a history in queueing theory, in two currencies. Counting came first. Nudge \cite{grosof2021nudge} lets a small arriving job swap past the job at the tail of the queue, Nudge-$K$ \cite{vanhoudt2022nudgek} lets it pass up to $K$ others while no job is passed more than once, and the line continues with Nudge*(M) \cite{charlet2026nudgestarm}; these are analysed in steady state under Poisson arrivals with light-tailed or phase-type sizes, and yield, for suitable parameters, stochastic dominance over first-come first-served. Because a job is passed at most once, Nudge implies on every sample path a per-job additive bound of one small-job length. That implication is our own reading and not a claim of \cite{grosof2021nudge}, whose analysis is stochastic by design, and we do not claim that form of result as new. The finite-skip models of WCFS restrict service to the oldest $n$ jobs and add work conservation and non-idling \cite{grosof2022wcfs}; their Theorem~2 brackets the mean response time within additive constants of the M/G/1 first-come first-served expression at every load, and not only in a heavy-traffic limit. The same positional notion is a proof device in fluid-limit analyses of inference serving \cite{dai2025llmthroughput}. Work is the second currency, and it is not ours either. Yu and Scully \cite{yu2024boost} analyse $\gamma$-Boost, which serves in order of arrival time minus a size-dependent boost, and define the \emph{crossing work} seen by a tagged job as the work of later arrivals whose boosted arrival time precedes its own; their Lemma~3.3 bounds that job's response time above and below in terms of the stationary workload at its boosted arrival instant and of crossing-work terms, for preemptive and non-preemptive Boost alike. Yu, Harlev, Adakroy and Scully \cite{yu2025twotraffics} carry crossing work to $k$ servers, where the job a non-preemptive server is already running enters the bound, and take first-come first-served as the policy to beat. Both are stationary results for light-tailed sizes aimed at the asymptotic tail constant; neither assumes a limit on service times, and neither gives a deterministic constant valid on every sample path, a converse, or a rule that enforces anything at run time.

Our contribution is therefore a conjunction and not a new currency. The identity is two-sided and holds on an arbitrary arrival sequence with arbitrary service times, under no distributional assumption; the constant $(2-2/k)L$ is exact for $k$ non-preemptive servers, as is the $(3-2/k)L$ of the wrapper; the converse makes a work budget necessary and not merely sufficient, up to an additive $O(kL)$; and Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} enforces the bound online around any base policy, learned ones included, under predictions that may be arbitrarily wrong. The additive shape and the constants are familiar on their own. Worst-case fair weighted fair queueing bounds a session's service in both directions against the ideal fluid reference, falling behind it by at most one maximum packet and running ahead of it by less than one of the session's own \cite{bennett1996wf2q}; the error term in the packet scale rate guarantee for expedited forwarding is the transmission time of one maximum-size lower-priority packet, and exists because that packet cannot be preempted \cite{bennett2002psrg}; additive per-individual bounds against a reference discipline are older still \cite{parekh1993gps,parekh1994gps}. The coefficient $2 - 1/k$ of Graham's list-scheduling bound has the same shape as the $(2-2/k)$ and $(3-2/k)$ above, and the additive form, $(1-1/k)$ times the length of the last task to finish, appears inside his proof \cite{graham1969bounds}; non-preemptive real-time feasibility conditions charge a whole uninterrupted execution over the interval at issue for the same reason \cite{jeffay1991nonpreemptive}. What we prove is not a conservation law in the sense of Kleinrock \cite{kleinrock1965conservation} and the achievable-region work after it, where a weighted sum of mean waiting times is invariant across a class of disciplines in steady state; the sample-path members of that family \cite{green2000samplepath,eltaha2017workload} are aggregate or class-level identities, not per-job comparisons against a named reference. The law does extend to several servers \cite{bartsch1978conservation}, but under non-preemptive priority rules with $k > 1$ it needs every class to share one service-time distribution \cite{federgruen1988mgc}, which is exactly what our model withholds. Lemma~\ref{lem:gap} is the quantitative substitute, bounding by $(k-1)L$ what workload invariance would otherwise supply for free. A pathwise work-difference bound of this shape appears in \cite[Lemma~2]{grosof2018srpt}, where the work of jobs of remaining size at most $x$ under $k$-server preemptive shortest-remaining-processing-time exceeds its single-server counterpart by at most $kx$; Lemma~\ref{lem:gap} differs in comparing two arbitrary non-preemptive work-conserving $k$-server policies, in bounding total unfinished work, and in being two-sided. Aging disciplines \cite{kleinrock1964delay,stanford2014apq} and the unfairness analyses of SRPT against processor sharing \cite{bansal2001srpt,wierman2003classifying} describe classes in steady state, not individual jobs. The multi-server accumulating priority queue orders service by a credit linear in waiting time, with exact class waiting-time distributions under exponential service \cite{sharif2014mapq,li2016mapq}; our $\mathrm{over}[q]$ accumulates overtaken work and carries a pathwise cap. EEVDF, a preemptive proportional-share scheduler, bounds a client's lag against the fluid ideal by $-r_{\max} < \mathrm{lag} < \max(r_{\max}, q)$, with $r_{\max}$ the largest request and $q$ the quantum, and by $\pm q$ when no request exceeds a quantum \cite{stoica1995eevdf}. The word needs one caution. In queueing theory \emph{overtaking} means departing before a customer who arrived earlier, counted across a network \cite{whitt1984overtaking} or in M/M/$c$ \cite{kim2016overtaking}; we instead measure the work that passes a waiting job. A single-server argument also stops working with several servers: the workload vector recursion is due to Kiefer and Wolfowitz \cite{kiefer1955queues}, and its minimality under first-come first-served, in the sense of weak majorization among service orders, is Daley's \cite{daley1987fcfs} and holds for systems in which arrivals are allocated to servers in a manner that does not depend on their service times. Our identity replaces that step with a quantitative comparison.

\subsection{Runtime prediction and scheduling in deployed systems}

Backfilling schedulers on high-performance computing systems have used predicted runtimes in place of user estimates for two decades \cite{tsafrir2007backfilling}, with predictions learned from past jobs and queue reordering tuned online \cite{gaussier2015backfilling,gaussier2018easytuning}; their resources are multi-dimensional and their reported metric is a mean bounded slowdown. Closest to our application setting, Zhang, Wu and Lu \cite{zhang2024ojscheduling} predict the running time of submitted code in a judging queue, cut the queue into fixed-size windows and dispatch in ascending predicted runtime within each window, reporting mean response time against first-come first-served. We are therefore not the first to schedule a judging queue by predicted cost. A fixed window does limit overtaking, but by position, and that work reports no per-job bound and no adversarial-prediction test in its abstract or its results, and does not describe its arrival process as recorded. In inference serving, where non-preemption is close to a hard constraint, recent systems learn a relative order instead of a length \cite{fu2024ltr} or target the tail because prediction-driven policies are fragile under distribution shift \cite{li2026tailaware,luo2025asrpt}; the first of those scores requests with the $\gamma$-Boost function of \cite{yu2024boost} and argues about starvation in that paper's crossing-work terms, which places it in the line discussed above. Production inference systems otherwise avoid starvation with aging counters and maximum waiting times, without proofs. Serverless platforms are in the same position. SFS orchestrates the Linux first-in first-out and fair schedulers from user space to approximate shortest-remaining-time-first, trading longer durations for long functions against the short ones \cite{fu2022sfs}, and ALPS learns a per-function priority rank and time-slice cap from an offline shortest-remaining-processing-time replay of recent traces and reuses the fair scheduler to keep long functions from starving \cite{fu2024alps}. Neither proves a bound. Our guard is a wrapper rather than a policy, so it can be placed around any of these schedulers, and its bound does not depend on which one is underneath.

\subsection{Time-varying and deadline-driven load}

That people concentrate their actions near a cutoff has been measured directly, in time management research \cite{konig2005deadline} and in conference registration data \cite{alfi2007deadline,alfi2009deadline}. The queueing consequences of an arrival rate that varies on the timescale of the service time are also well studied: peak congestion in a multi-server system lags the peak of the arrival rate \cite{massey1997peak}, and staffing rules that follow a time-varying rate rest on offered-load approximations rather than stationary formulas \cite{jennings1996staffing,green2007coping,feldman2008staffing}. These results say how many servers to provide. They give no waiting bound for an individual job and no guidance for a queue that has already formed, and we leave capacity fixed. The steady-state classified-priority formula that motivates ranking by predicted cost \cite{mitzenmacher2020misprediction,harcholbalter2013} does not survive contact with this load either; Section~\ref{sec:model} measures the mismatch, which is why we state the guarantee on sample paths and assume nothing about arrivals.

\subsection{Relational and tabular models for cost prediction}

Our comparison includes a heterogeneous graph neural network over users, tasks and assignments, in the relational graph convolution family \cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn,xu2019gin}. Two features of the published benchmarks make them a poor argument either way. In RelBench \cite{robinson2024relbench} and its successor \cite{gu2026relbenchv2}, the gradient-boosted baseline is fitted on the raw columns of the entity table alone, without hand-built aggregates over related tables, so the margin reported there is not a comparison against a tabular model carrying engineered history features. The same paper's study against a human data scientist is the closer comparison: the relational model matched or beat the expert on 11 of 15 tasks, while the authors record that regression tasks are where it struggles. Cost prediction is a regression task on entities with long individual histories, which places it in that region, and the evidence that tree ensembles remain strong on tabular data concerns single tables \cite{grinsztajn2022tabular}. We therefore settle the question inside our own protocol instead of importing a conclusion, with a tabular baseline that carries engineered causal history features \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, an ablation ladder that removes and then permutes the edges, and a comparison made on queueing outcomes rather than on AUROC alone. The negative result we report is about this task on these traces, where each user and each task already has enough history of its own; the only relational signal we find is on task types that have none yet.
