\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Scheduling with Predictions}

Algorithms with predictions are judged by consistency, how well they do when the
predictions are exact, and robustness, how well they do when the predictions are
arbitrarily wrong \cite{lykouris2021caching,purohit2018predictions}. In scheduling,
predicted sizes have been used to minimise the total completion time of jobs whose sizes
are unknown \cite{im2021nonclairvoyant}, learned machine weights to assign jobs online
\cite{lattanzi2020learnedweights}, and a predicted order of the jobs in place of their
sizes \cite{lindermayr2022permutation}. Queueing models treat the prediction as a random
variable \cite{mitzenmacher2020misprediction}, bound its error multiplicatively
\cite{scully2022uniform} or measure it as a distortion
\cite{azar2021flowtime,azar2022distortion}, and further work covers many-server limits
\cite{dong2025sjf}, the cost of obtaining predictions \cite{shahout2024skippredict} and
the worst slowdown of any job on a machine that may interrupt jobs
\cite{zhao2025fairpredictions}; Mitzenmacher and Shahout survey this line
\cite{mitzenmacher2025queueing}. These results bound a mean or a competitive ratio against
an optimal schedule, and the bound degrades with a measure of the prediction error. In
their terms, our guarantee is a robustness statement made for each job and on every
sample path: it holds for any predictions, but it bounds the loss against FCFS and does
not by itself promise a gain, which Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures.

\subsection{Per-Job Guarantees and Bounded Overtaking}

Schwiegelshohn and Yahyapour call a schedule $\lambda$-fair when later submissions cannot
increase a job's flow time by more than a factor $\lambda$, and bound fairness and
efficiency together for a policy that interrupts whole groups of processors
\cite{schwiegelshohn2000fairness}. Batch schedulers measure a job's delay against a fair
start time, obtained by simulating the schedule again without the later arrivals
\cite{sabin2004fairness,sabin2005unfairness}. Conservative backfilling gives every queued
job a reservation that later jobs cannot delay \cite{mualem2001backfilling,lindsay2013backfilling},
slack-based backfilling lets later jobs delay a job only within a slack fixed at
submission \cite{talby1999slack}, and a preemptive variant of the Extensible Argonne
Scheduling sYstem (EASY) backfilling \cite{lifka1995easy} kills and restarts the backfilled
jobs that would delay a higher-priority one \cite{yuan2010pveasy}. These guarantees rest on
reservations or on interrupting jobs; ours is additive, needs no interruption and is
measured against FCFS on the same input.

Bounded overtaking has been studied in two units. The first is a count: Nudge lets a small
arriving job swap past the job at the tail of the queue \cite{grosof2021nudge}, Nudge-$K$
lets it pass up to $K$ others while no job is passed more than once
\cite{vanhoudt2022nudgek}, with later refinements \cite{charlet2026nudgestarm}, and finite-skip models serve only among
the oldest $n$ waiting jobs \cite{grosof2022wcfs}. The second is work: $\gamma$-Boost
measures the work of later arrivals that are served ahead of a tagged job
\cite{yu2024boost,yu2025twotraffics}. These analyses assume stationary Poisson arrivals and
target means or tail asymptotics, not a cap enforced on every sample path. Our wrapper has
the architecture of Load Balancing Guardrails \cite{grosof2019guardrails}, which leaves an
arbitrary dispatching policy free except where a constraint must be enforced, although
that work optimises mean response time in heavy traffic. Additive bounds against a
reference discipline are standard in packet scheduling
\cite{demers1989fq,parekh1993gps,bennett1996wf2q,stoica1995eevdf}, and the coefficient of
Graham's list-scheduling bound \cite{graham1969bounds} has the same shape as the $(2-2/k)$
of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. Supplementary Section~S9 compares these guarantees in detail.

\subsection{Prediction-Driven Scheduling in Deployed Systems}

Backfilling schedulers for high-performance computing replace users' runtime estimates by
predictions learned from past jobs, and report mean slowdown or mean wait
\cite{tsafrir2007backfilling,gaussier2015backfilling,gaussier2018easytuning}. Closest to our
first case, Zhang, Wu and Lu predict the running time of submitted programs in a judging
queue and order the jobs by prediction inside fixed-size windows
\cite{zhang2024ojscheduling}, which limits overtaking by position; they report no per-job
bound. Servers for large language models, which cannot cheaply interrupt a request, learn a
relative order of the requests \cite{fu2024ltr} or target the latency tail, because
prediction-driven policies are fragile when the workload shifts \cite{li2026tailaware};
others promote a request once its wait passes a threshold \cite{wu2023fastserve} or a
multiple of its service time \cite{luo2025autellix}. Serverless platforms approximate
shortest remaining time first from user space \cite{fu2022sfs} or learn a priority for
each function \cite{fu2024alps}. None of these states a bound for individual jobs. A
maximum waiting time does carry one, of the same form as ours
(Section~\ref{sec:clock}), and our guard can be placed around any of these schedulers.

\subsection{Deadline-Driven Load}

People concentrate their actions before a cutoff \cite{konig2005deadline} and register in
bursts before a deadline \cite{alfi2007deadline,alfi2009deadline}. When the arrival rate
changes as fast as jobs are served, congestion peaks after the arrivals do
\cite{massey1997peak}, and staffing rules need time-varying approximations
\cite{jennings1996staffing}. These results size the capacity; they give no bound for an
individual job once a queue has formed, and we keep the capacity fixed.

\subsection{Relational Models for Cost Prediction}

A job's cost may depend on its user, its task and the relations between them, which is the
setting of relational deep learning. On the RelBench benchmarks, graph models over linked
tables beat gradient-boosted trees fitted on the columns of a single table
\cite{robinson2024relbench}, while on single tables tree ensembles still outperform deep
models \cite{grinsztajn2022tabular}. We therefore compare a heterogeneous graph neural
network over users, tasks and assignments \cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn,xu2019gin} with
gradient-boosted trees \cite{ke2017lightgbm} that carry engineered history features, and we
judge them by the waits they produce in the queue as well as by their prediction scores.
