\section{Problem Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Jobs, Servers and Dispatch}

The judging queue of the introduction becomes a finite set of $n$ jobs served by $k$
identical servers. We use \emph{server}
throughout for what a deployment would call an executor, a judge host or a worker, and
\emph{job} for what a platform would call a submission, an invocation or a run.
Job $i$ arrives at time $a_i \ge 0$ and requires $C_i$ units of service. Service is non-preemptive, so a
server that starts a job keeps it until the job finishes or until it has been running
for the per-job time limit $L$, at which point the system stops it. Throughout, $C_i$
denotes executed work, so $0 < C_i \le L$ by construction and a job stopped at the
limit contributes exactly $L$. An enforced timeout ends such a job without a
successful result; the waiting-time guarantee does not bound that loss of completion
quality. Observed maxima and analysis truncations in a trace are distinguished from
enforced timeouts in Section~\ref{sec:data}. One quantity carries three names in this paper: we write
\emph{cost} in the application sections, \emph{service} or \emph{service time} in the
analysis, and \emph{work} when several jobs are summed, and all three denote $C_i$.
Two further words are fixed here because the application sections need them. A
\emph{task} is the exercise or problem a job is an attempt at, so many jobs share one
task; a \emph{run} is the unit of work of a continuous-integration pool, and is a job in
the sense above.

Servers are work-conserving, meaning that no server idles at an instant at which some
job waits. Jobs carry a rank, the total order induced by the pair (arrival time, input
index); we write $j < i$ for ``$j$ has the smaller rank'', which implies
$a_j \le a_i$. Events occurring at a common instant are processed as completions,
then arrivals, then dispatches. A policy is any rule that names one waiting job and
dispatches it, whenever a server is free and at least one job waits. It may be
randomised, learned, priority-driven, or fed by predictions of arbitrary quality. We
assume nothing else about it.

Arrival times are arbitrary. We place no stochastic assumption on $a_i$, on $C_i$, or
on the dependence between them: an external calendar generates the load we care about,
and that load is neither stationary nor Poisson.

\subsection{What the Scheduler Knows}

The service time $C_i$ is hidden at arrival. The scheduler reads it at the instant $i$
completes and not before. Any service that runs submitted work without looking inside it
first is in that position. Available at $a_i$ are a feature vector $x_i$ and a
score $\hat{C}_i$ computed from it.

Visibility is causal, and we define it by result availability instead of timestamp
order. Write $\mathrm{done}_j$ for the instant at which the outcome of job $j$ became
readable and $\delta \ge 0$ for the delay with which outcomes reach the feature store.
Then
\[
  \text{information about } j \text{ may enter } x_i
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathrm{done}_j + \delta \le a_i .
\]
Historical features are recomputed under the chosen release rule rather than assembled
once and masked afterwards. The original prediction study in
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} uses the source platform's result clock. In a replayed
queue, an endogenous result instead becomes available at
$\mathrm{done}^{P}_j=a_j+W_P[j]+C_j$. Source-clock causality therefore does not imply
availability under policy $P$: a recorded result may already be visible while its
replayed job is still waiting. The headline of Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}
therefore uses an exact protocol: each policy's replay is repeated, leaving out every
result that the replay says was not yet complete when the job that uses it arrived,
until no score uses an unavailable result. Two references sit beside it. A conservative
protocol restricts history to replayed submissions in the same class-term copy, one
class of one term as placed in the overlay, and delays every result by a fixed lag; a
static control uses no outcome history. Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility} says how
availability is checked and separates external history from queueing exposure inside
the same copy. The guard theorem does not depend on which scores these protocols
produce.

\subsection{Waiting Time, the FCFS Reference and the Service Guarantee}

Let $s^P_i$ be the start time of job $i$ under policy $P$ and
$W_P[i] = s^P_i - a_i$ its waiting time, which excludes service. FCFS is the policy
that always dispatches the waiting job of smallest rank, and that dispatches in
increasing rank when several servers become free at the same instant. We run it on the
same arrival sequence, with the same service times and the same $k$, so that the
comparison is pathwise rather than distributional. The per-job excess is
\[
  \mathrm{excess}_P[i] \;=\; W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] .
\]

Two further quantities carry the analysis. Writing $j \prec i$ for ``$j$ is dispatched
before $i$'' in the dispatch sequence,
\[
  \mathrm{In}_i \;=\; \sum_{j > i,\; j \prec i} C_j ,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Out}_i \;=\; \sum_{j < i,\; i \prec j} C_j ,
\]
the work that overtook $i$ and the work that $i$ overtook. Section~\ref{sec:theory}
relates them to $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$.

We say that a policy $P$ offers a service guarantee $G$ if
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ for every job $i$, on every arrival sequence, for every
vector of service times bounded by $L$, and for every score, adversarial scores
included. Two words are kept apart from here on: the \emph{promise} is the number $G$
the operator states, and the \emph{guarantee} is the proved statement that
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ holds for every job. An operator chooses a promise; a
theorem supplies a guarantee. The statement is per job and holds on the realised sample
path, in no sense in distribution or on average, and it does not assume that the score
is any good. Packet scheduling states per-individual additive bounds against a
reference discipline~\cite{parekh1993gps}, and batch schedulers measure the same
difference after the fact as a fairness
diagnostic~\cite{sabin2004fairness,sabin2005unfairness}. Those authors define the fair
start time in two variants; we use the relaxed one, first-come first-served with no
backfilling. The scheduling-with-predictions literature does something different: it
compares against an optimal policy in an average sense, under a bound on the prediction
error~\cite{purohit2018predictions,scully2022uniform}.

We take FCFS as the reference because it is what these services deploy by default, so
the excess is a quantity an operator can be held to. It also has a known extremal
property for the unfinished-work vector in a multiserver
system: the FCFS workload vector is minimal in the weak-majorisation order among
systems whose assignment of arrivals to servers does not depend on service
times~\cite{daley1987fcfs}, a hypothesis narrower than work conservation alone. That
extremality is why single-server arguments do not transfer
unchanged to $k > 1$.

\subsection{Metrics}

The primary metric is the 99th percentile of waiting time inside deadline windows, and
we write $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ for it, the symbol the tables carry as a column
head. A
deadline window is the 24 hours before a deadline; a job belongs to the window if
and only if its arrival falls inside it, and a job that arrives inside but completes
after the window closes is still counted. We fix this choice before any sealed part of
a trace is opened.

Four secondary quantities go with it. The first is the mean wait over all jobs. The
second is the maximum of $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$, the quantity a service guarantee
bounds. The third is the \emph{harm}: the largest excess over FCFS among the jobs FCFS
would have started within $1$ s, that is, among the jobs FCFS would not have delayed at
all. A guarantee $G$ says nothing about how that excess is distributed, and the harm is
what an operator's users complain about. The fourth is the \emph{firing rate}, the
share of dispatch epochs at which the guard of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} overrides
the base policy, weighted by queue length. We also report the worst wait among heavy
jobs, heavy being the label Section~\ref{sec:prediction} defines.

Improvements are also expressed as the fraction of the gap closed,
\[
  \frac{\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{P}}
       {\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{\mathrm{SJF}}} ,
\]
where SJF sorts waiting jobs by their true service times. SJF on true sizes is a
reference policy and not an optimum. It is not deployable, since $C_i$ is unknown at
arrival, and it does not minimise any of the metrics above on a non-preemptive
multiserver system. The ratio is unstable when its denominator is small, so we mark
such configurations and print the two absolute waits next to every ratio. The fraction
of the gap closed is not a percentage reduction in waiting time and we do not report
it as one.

\subsection{Steady-State Models and This Load}

Stationary queueing models give closed forms for the mean wait of a non-preemptive
priority class~\cite{harcholbalter2013} and would let class thresholds be placed
analytically. They need a stationary arrival process, which deadline-driven load does
not supply. On a synthetic Poisson stream the formula agrees with simulation to
\devnum{0.4\%}, but on the same workload at its recorded timestamps the measured wait is
\devnum{10.7}--\devnum{13.7} times what it predicts (Supplementary Section~S2.5). We
therefore use steady-state expressions as motivation only, and every promise here is a
per-job statement on the realised sample path (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The mechanism
that makes reordering useful is the three-job arithmetic of the introduction, where
total work is unchanged while one long job moves back a little and several short ones
move forward a lot.
