\section{Problem Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Jobs, Servers and Dispatch}

A finite set of $n$ jobs is served by $k$ identical servers. We use \emph{server}
throughout for what a deployment would call an executor, a judge host or a worker, and
\emph{job} for what a platform would call a submission, an invocation or a run.
Job $i$ arrives at time $a_i \ge 0$ and requires $C_i$ units of service. Service is non-preemptive, so a
server that starts a job keeps it until the job finishes or until it has been running
for the per-job time limit $L$, at which point the system stops it. Throughout, $C_i$
denotes executed work, so $0 < C_i \le L$ by construction and a job stopped at the
limit contributes exactly $L$.

Servers are work-conserving, meaning that no server idles at an instant at which some
job waits. Jobs carry a rank, the total order induced by the pair (arrival time, input
index); we write $j < i$ for ``$j$ has the smaller rank'', which implies
$a_j \le a_i$. Events occurring at a common instant are processed as completions,
then arrivals, then dispatches. A policy is any rule that, whenever a server is free
and at least one job waits, names one waiting job and dispatches it; it may be
randomised, learned, priority-driven, or fed by predictions of arbitrary quality, and
we assume nothing else about it.

Arrival times are arbitrary. We place no stochastic assumption on $a_i$, on $C_i$, or
on the dependence between them, because the load we are concerned with is generated by
an external calendar and is neither stationary nor Poisson.

\subsection{What the Scheduler Knows}

The service time $C_i$ is hidden at arrival. The scheduler reads it at the instant $i$
completes and not before, which is the situation of any service that runs submitted
work without inspecting it first. Available at $a_i$ are a feature vector $x_i$ and a
score $\hat{C}_i$ computed from it.

Visibility is causal, and we define it by result availability rather than by timestamp
order. Write $\mathrm{done}_j$ for the instant at which the outcome of job $j$ became
readable and $\delta \ge 0$ for the delay with which outcomes reach the feature store.
Then
\[
  \text{information about } j \text{ may enter } x_i
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathrm{done}_j + \delta \le a_i .
\]
Every historical feature is recomputed under this rule rather than assembled once and
masked afterwards; Section~\ref{sec:prediction} gives the procedure and the
sensitivity to $\delta$.

\subsection{Waiting Time, the FCFS Reference and the Service Guarantee}

Let $s^P_i$ be the start time of job $i$ under policy $P$ and
$W_P[i] = s^P_i - a_i$ its waiting time, which excludes service. FCFS is the policy
that always dispatches the waiting job of smallest rank, and that dispatches in
increasing rank when several servers become free at the same instant. We run it on the
same arrival sequence, with the same service times and the same $k$, so that the
comparison is pathwise rather than distributional. The per-job excess is
\[
  \mathrm{excess}_P[i] \;=\; W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] .
\]

Two further quantities carry the analysis. Writing $j \prec i$ for ``$j$ is dispatched
before $i$'' in the dispatch sequence,
\[
  \mathrm{In}_i \;=\; \sum_{j > i,\; j \prec i} C_j ,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Out}_i \;=\; \sum_{j < i,\; i \prec j} C_j ,
\]
the work that overtook $i$ and the work that $i$ overtook. Section~\ref{sec:theory}
relates them to $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$.

We say that a policy $P$ offers a service guarantee $G$ if
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ for every job $i$, on every arrival sequence, for every
vector of service times bounded by $L$, and for every score, adversarial scores
included. The statement is per job and holds on the realised sample path rather than
in distribution or on average, and it does not presume that the score is any good. A
per-individual additive bound relative to a reference discipline is familiar from
packet scheduling~\cite{parekh1993gps}, and the same difference has been measured
after the fact as a fairness diagnostic in batch
schedulers~\cite{sabin2005unfairness}. The robustness results in the
scheduling-with-predictions literature are of a different kind, comparing against an
optimal policy in an average sense under a bound on the prediction
error~\cite{purohit2018predictions,scully2022uniform}.

We take FCFS as the reference because it is what these services deploy by default, so
the excess is a quantity an operator can be held to. It also has known extremal
properties for the unfinished-work vector in a multiserver
system~\cite{daley1987fcfs}, which is why single-server arguments do not transfer
unchanged to $k > 1$.

\subsection{Metrics}

The primary metric is the 99th percentile of waiting time inside deadline windows. A
deadline window is the 24 hours preceding a deadline; a job belongs to the window if
and only if its arrival falls inside it, and a job that arrives inside but completes
after the window closes is still counted. We fix this choice before any sealed part of
a trace is opened. We also report four secondary quantities. The mean wait over all
jobs. The maximum of $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$, which is what a service guarantee bounds.
The \emph{harm}, the largest excess over FCFS among the jobs FCFS would have started
within $1$ s, that is, among the jobs FCFS would not have delayed at all; a guarantee
$G$ says nothing about how that excess is distributed, and the harm is where an
operator feels it. The \emph{firing rate}, the share of dispatch epochs at which the
guard of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} overrides the base policy, weighted by queue
length. We also report the worst wait among heavy jobs, heavy being the label
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} defines.

Improvements are also expressed as the fraction of the gap closed,
\[
  \frac{\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{P}}
       {\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{\mathrm{SJF}}} ,
\]
where SJF sorts waiting jobs by their true service times. SJF on true sizes is a
reference policy and not an optimum. It is not deployable, since $C_i$ is unknown at
arrival, and it does not minimise any of the metrics above on a non-preemptive
multiserver system. The ratio is unstable when its denominator is small, so we mark
such configurations and print the two absolute waits next to every ratio. The fraction
of the gap closed is not a percentage reduction in waiting time and we do not report
it as one.

\subsection{Why the Design Is Not Based on a Steady-State Model}

Stationary queueing models give closed forms for the mean wait of a non-preemptive
priority class~\cite{harcholbalter2013} and would let class thresholds be placed
analytically. They need a stationary arrival process, which deadline-driven load does
not supply. Rather than assert that the mismatch matters, we measured it. On
a synthetic Poisson stream whose service distribution matches the first two moments of
the real one, the formula agrees with simulation to \devnum{0.4\%}. On the same
workload replayed at its recorded timestamps, the measured wait is
\devnum{10.7}--\devnum{13.7} times what the formula predicts on the development trace.
A threshold tuned on the formula would be tuned against a number that is off by an
order of magnitude.

We therefore use steady-state expressions as motivation only, and every promise made
in this paper is a per-job statement on the realised sample path
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The mechanism that makes reordering worth doing is the
three-job arithmetic of the introduction, where total work is unchanged while one long
job moves back a little and several short ones move forward a lot.
