\section{Problem Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Jobs, Servers and Dispatch}

The judging queue of the introduction becomes a finite set of $n$ jobs served by $k$
identical servers. We use \emph{server}
throughout for what a deployment would call an executor, a judge host or a worker, and
\emph{job} for what a platform would call a submission, an invocation or a run.
Job $i$ arrives at time $a_i \ge 0$ and requires $C_i$ units of service. Service is non-preemptive, so a
server that starts a job keeps it until the job finishes or until it has been running
for the per-job time limit $L$, at which point the system stops it. Throughout, $C_i$
denotes executed work, so $0 < C_i \le L$ by construction and a job stopped at the
limit contributes exactly $L$. An enforced timeout ends such a job without a successful result, and the waiting-time guarantee does not bound that loss of completion
quality. In Section~\ref{sec:data} we distinguish observed maxima and analysis truncations in a
trace from enforced timeouts. One quantity carries three names, \emph{cost} in the application sections, \emph{service}
or \emph{service time} in the analysis, and \emph{work} when several jobs are summed, all
three denoting $C_i$.
Two further words are fixed here because the application sections need them. A
\emph{task} is the exercise or problem a job is an attempt at, so many jobs share one task. Similarly, a \emph{run} is the unit of work of a continuous-integration pool, and is a job in
the sense above.

Servers are work-conserving, meaning that no server idles at an instant at which some
job waits. Jobs carry a rank, the total order induced by the pair (arrival time, input index), and we write $j < i$ for ``$j$ has the smaller rank'', which implies
$a_j \le a_i$. At a common instant, events are processed as completions, then arrivals, then
dispatches. A policy is any rule that names one waiting job and
dispatches it, whenever a server is free and at least one job waits. In particular, it may be randomised, learned, priority-driven, or fed by predictions of arbitrary quality, and we assume nothing else about it.

Arrival times are arbitrary, and we place no stochastic assumption on $a_i$, on $C_i$, or
on the dependence between them, because an external calendar generates the load we care
about and that load is neither stationary nor Poisson.

\subsection{What the Scheduler Knows}

At arrival the service time $C_i$ is hidden, and the scheduler reads it at the instant
$i$ completes and not before. Any service that runs submitted work without looking inside it
first is in that position. Available at $a_i$ are a feature vector $x_i$ and a
score $\hat{C}_i$ computed from it.

Visibility is causal, and we define it by result availability instead of timestamp
order. Write $\mathrm{done}_j$ for the instant at which the outcome of job $j$ became
readable and $\delta \ge 0$ for the delay with which outcomes reach the feature store.
Then
\[
  \text{information about } j \text{ may enter } x_i
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \mathrm{done}_j + \delta \le a_i .
\]
Historical features are recomputed under the chosen release rule rather than assembled
once and masked afterwards. In Section~\ref{sec:prediction}, the original prediction study uses the source platform's
result clock. In a replayed
queue, an endogenous result instead becomes available at
$\mathrm{done}^{P}_j=a_j+W_P[j]+C_j$. Hence source-clock causality does not imply availability under policy $P$, since a
recorded result may already be visible while its
replayed job is still waiting. The headline of Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility} therefore uses an exact protocol,
in which each policy's replay is repeated, leaving out every
result that the replay says was not yet complete when the job that uses it arrived,
until no score uses an unavailable result. Beside it sit two references, a conservative protocol that restricts history to replayed submissions in the same class-term copy, one
class of one term as placed in the overlay, and delays every result by a fixed lag, and a static control that uses no outcome history. Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility} says how
availability is checked and separates external history from queueing exposure inside
the same copy. The guard theorem does not depend on which scores these protocols produce.

\subsection{Waiting Time, the FCFS Reference and the Service Guarantee}

Let $s^P_i$ be the start time of job $i$ under policy $P$ and
$W_P[i] = s^P_i - a_i$ its waiting time, which excludes service. FCFS is the policy
that always dispatches the waiting job of smallest rank, and that dispatches in
increasing rank when several servers become free at the same instant. We run it on the
same arrival sequence, with the same service times and the same $k$, so that the
comparison is pathwise rather than distributional. For each job $i$ we define the excess
\[
  \mathrm{excess}_P[i] \;=\; W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] .
\]

Writing $j \prec i$ for ``$j$ is dispatched before $i$'' in the dispatch sequence, we
define two further quantities that carry the analysis,
\[
  \mathrm{In}_i \;=\; \sum_{j > i,\; j \prec i} C_j ,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Out}_i \;=\; \sum_{j < i,\; i \prec j} C_j ,
\]
the work that overtook $i$ and the work that $i$ overtook, which
Section~\ref{sec:theory} relates to $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$.

We say that a policy $P$ offers a service guarantee $G$ if
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ for every job $i$, on every arrival sequence, for every
vector of service times bounded by $L$, and for every score, adversarial scores
included. From here on the \emph{promise} is the number $G$
the operator states, and the \emph{guarantee} is the proved statement that
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ holds for every job, so an operator chooses a promise and a theorem supplies a
guarantee. Thus the statement is
per job and holds on the realised sample
path, in no sense in distribution or on average, and it does not assume that the score
is any good. Packet scheduling states per-individual additive bounds against a reference
discipline~\cite{parekh1993gps}, and batch schedulers measure the same
difference after the fact as a fairness
diagnostic~\cite{sabin2004fairness,sabin2005unfairness}. Those authors define the fair
start time by re-simulating the scheduler under test without the later arrivals, in two
variants, and in the relaxed one the job may not start before any job queued ahead of it.
Our reference runs first-come first-served for every job instead, so the two coincide
when the scheduler under test is first-come first-served. By contrast, the scheduling-with-predictions literature compares against an optimal
policy, with a competitive ratio that degrades with the
prediction error~\cite{purohit2018predictions} or an average-case ratio under a bound on
that error~\cite{scully2022uniform}.

We take FCFS as the reference because it is what these services deploy by default, so
the excess is a quantity an operator can be held to. In addition, it has a known extremal property for the unfinished-work vector in a
multiserver system, since the FCFS workload vector is minimal in the weak-majorisation order among
systems whose assignment of arrivals to servers does not depend on service
times~\cite{daley1987fcfs}, a hypothesis narrower than work conservation alone. Single-server arguments therefore do not transfer unchanged to $k > 1$.

\subsection{Metrics}

The primary metric is the 99th percentile of waiting time inside deadline windows, and
we write $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$ for it, the symbol the tables carry as a column
head. A
deadline window is the 24 hours before a deadline. Membership is decided by arrival, so a job that arrives
inside the window but completes after it closes is still counted. We fix this choice before any sealed part of
a trace is opened.

Four secondary quantities go with it, the first being the mean wait over all jobs. The
second is the maximum of $\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$, the quantity a service guarantee
bounds. The third is the \emph{harm}, the largest excess over FCFS among the jobs FCFS
would have started within $1$ s, that is, among the jobs FCFS would not have delayed at
all. A guarantee $G$ says nothing about how that excess is distributed, and the harm is
what an operator's users complain about. The fourth is the \emph{firing rate}, the
share of dispatch epochs at which the guard of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} overrides
the base policy, weighted by queue length. We also report the worst wait among heavy
jobs, heavy being the label Section~\ref{sec:prediction} defines.

We also express improvements as the fraction of the gap closed,
\[
  \frac{M_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - M_{P}}
       {M_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - M_{\mathrm{SJF}}} ,
\]
where $M_Q$ is the primary metric, $\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{dl}}$, under policy $Q$,
and SJF sorts waiting jobs by their true service times. SJF on true sizes is a
reference policy and not an optimum. In particular, it is not deployable, since $C_i$ is unknown at arrival, and it does not minimise any of the metrics above on a non-preemptive
multiserver system. When its denominator is small the ratio is unstable, so we mark
such configurations and print the two absolute waits next to every ratio. The fraction
of the gap closed is not a percentage reduction in waiting time and we do not report
it as one.

\subsection{Steady-State Models and This Load}

Stationary queueing models give closed forms for the mean wait of a non-preemptive
priority class~\cite[pp.~499--507]{harcholbalter2013} and would let class thresholds be placed analytically, but they need a stationary arrival
process, which deadline-driven load does
not supply. On a synthetic Poisson stream the formula agrees with simulation to
\devnum{0.4\%}, but on the same workload at its recorded timestamps the measured wait is
\devnum{10.7}--\devnum{13.7} times what it predicts (Supplementary Section~S2.5). We
therefore use steady-state expressions as motivation only, and every promise here is a
per-job statement on the realised sample path (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The mechanism
that makes reordering useful is the three-job arithmetic of the introduction, where
total work is unchanged while one long job moves back a little and several short ones
move forward a lot.
