\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A shared execution service runs jobs on behalf of many users on a fixed pool of $k$ servers. A job that has started is not interrupted: it runs until it finishes or until it reaches a per-job time limit $L$. Serverless function platforms, build and test farms for continuous integration, batch inference queues, and the judging back ends of programming-assignment platforms are all organised this way \cite{wasik2018ojsurvey}. Operators document the consequence themselves: a contest judging system recommends one judging host per twenty teams and notes that with fewer hosts the waiting time grows \cite{domjudge_manual}, and the maintainers of a university autograder replayed the busiest four hours before a common deadline and found that one submission looping until its resource limit took a grader out of circulation and delayed the graders behind it \cite{peveler2019sandbox}. Two properties of the offered load make such a service slow down when it is used most. Arrivals follow an external calendar, so they are neither stationary nor independent; the same concentration near a fixed cutoff has been measured directly \cite{konig2005deadline,alfi2007deadline,alfi2009deadline}. And job costs are heavy-tailed: on the serverless invocation trace we use \cite{azure2021trace,zhang2021harvested}, the longest \devnum{1\%} of invocations account for \devnum{44\%} of the total work, and on the judging traces, after the platform time limit is applied, the longest \devnum{1\%} still account for \devnum{37.5\%}. A few very long jobs therefore sit in front of many short ones exactly when the queue is longest.

The mechanism we exploit is visible in a queue of three jobs on one server (Figure~\ref{fig:threejob}). Job $A$ loops forever and is stopped by the 60-second limit; jobs $B$ and $C$ take 0.2 seconds each. In arrival order the mean wait is 40.1 seconds; shortest first it is 0.2 seconds, at a cost of 0.4 seconds to $A$. The server performs the same total work in both orders. This is arithmetic rather than an experiment, and it rests on one assumption: that the scheduler knows, before $A$ starts, that $A$ will be long. That assumption raises three questions, and the paper is organised around them. Can the cost of a job be estimated at the moment it arrives, from history that is causally visible at that instant? Given estimates, what should a non-preemptive scheduler rank by? And what happens to an individual job when the estimates are wrong?

On the first, arrival-time history is enough to tell the heavy jobs from the rest. Using only features whose values were already available at the arrival instant, gradient-boosted trees \cite{ke2017lightgbm} reach an AUROC of \devnum{0.93}, \devnum{0.93} and \devnum{0.98} for identifying heavy jobs on the three platforms we study. We compare three families of predictor under one visibility protocol and one split: tabular models, a sequence model over each user's history, and a heterogeneous graph neural network over the user, task and assignment entities \cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn}. The graph model does not improve on the tabular one here. An ablation that removes every edge, and then one that permutes the edges at random, leaves its classification scores where they were, which places the signal in each entity's own history rather than in the relational structure. We report this as a property of this prediction task, not as a general statement about relational models.

On the second question, the ranking score follows from non-preemption. Once a job starts, its whole realised cost is added to the wait of every job behind it, so waiting time is additive in realised cost and jobs should be ordered by the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[C \mid x]$. A point prediction fitted with squared error on a log scale estimates a conditional geometric mean instead; the two differ by a conditional variance term, and that term is concentrated on the same small fraction of heavy jobs that the schedule is trying to move. The errors are also asymmetric. Replacing the worst \devnum{1\%} of under-estimates by true costs closes \devnum{0.91} of the remaining gap to a scheduler that knows true costs, while replacing the worst \devnum{1\%} of over-estimates closes about \devnum{0.25}. Putting a heavy job early is the expensive mistake.

The third question decides whether an operator would run any of this. Ordering by prediction improves the bulk of the distribution and can damage the tail: in our busiest configuration one job waited \devnum{5{,}905} seconds longer than it would have under first-come first-served. The robustness results we are aware of in the scheduling-with-predictions literature bound a mean relative to an optimal or shortest-remaining-processing-time reference, and the constant-factor ones require the prediction error to be bounded multiplicatively, which is not a property anyone can promise about a deployed predictor \cite{scully2022uniform,mitzenmacher2025queueing}. We change the reference point instead of strengthening the assumption, and bound, for each job separately, how much longer it waits than it would have waited under first-come first-served on the same arrival sequence, assuming nothing about the arrival process and nothing about prediction quality. The mechanism is an overtake budget: for every waiting job we account the total work of later-ordered jobs that have completed while it waited, and when that account reaches a budget the job is dispatched next. The guard wraps an arbitrary base scheduler, including a learned one, and touches only the dispatch decision. It is one mechanism with one parameter that matters for the promise, a cap $B_{\max}$ on the budget; what the budget does below that cap is a design freedom that changes how much of the base policy's benefit survives and which jobs pay for it, and changes the promise not at all.

Budgeting work rather than counting overtakes is forced rather than chosen. We prove an identity that holds for every non-preemptive, work-conserving $k$-server policy and every arrival sequence. A job's delay relative to first-come first-served equals its net overtaken work divided by $k$, to within $(2-2/k)L$, with equality when $k = 1$. That constant is the exact supremum of the error, approached by an explicit family and never attained, and its two directions are not alike: upward the victim really does wait $(2-2/k)L$ longer than its net overtaken work predicts, while downward about half of the extremal value is the bookkeeping convention that charges an overtaker dispatched in the victim's own phase, which executes nothing while the victim waits, and there the victim's own excess is negative. The identity does not refer to the policy, and it cuts both ways. Bounding overtaken work is sufficient for a per-job guarantee, and any policy that offers such a guarantee must bound it, so mechanisms that limit the number of overtakes are neither sufficient nor necessary for it. The guarantee is denominated in units of $L$: it says something when $L$ is comparable to the waits an operator cares about and nothing when it is not, and a computing farm whose jobs run for days is a case where the method should not be used, which we include.

We make four contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The net-overtake identity just stated, with the constant $(2-2/k)L$ shown to be an exact supremum, approached and never attained, and a corollary in the other direction: a bound on overtaken work is necessary for any per-job guarantee of this kind.
  \item An overtake-budget guard. It wraps any base scheduler and bounds every job's wait against first-come first-served, for $k$ servers, arbitrary arrivals and arbitrary predictions. The bound reads the budget only through a cap: any budget rule with $0 \le \mathrm{budget} \le B_{\max}$ gives the same guarantee, so the operator sets $B_{\max} = k(G - (3-2/k)L)$ from a promise $G$ in seconds, with no data, and is then free in how the budget is spent below it. The additive constant $(3-2/k)L$, beyond the budget term, is an exact supremum, never attained, and it grows with $k$ from $L$ at $k = 1$ towards $3L$; no analysis of this mechanism can improve it. One shape of budget, the one whose allowance grows with the wait a job has already had, carries a multiplicative bound as well. We state which assumptions can be dropped, and show by counterexample that work conservation cannot.
  \item A comparison of cost predictors on causally visible history, covering tabular, sequence and heterogeneous graph models, with two conclusions that carry into the scheduler: rank by expected cost rather than by a point or log-scale prediction, and treat the error as asymmetric.
  \item An evaluation on real logs from two programming-assignment judging platforms \cite{codebench_dataset,accoding2024} and a serverless invocation trace \cite{azure2021trace}, with every simulated job checked against the theoretical bound, together with workloads on which the method does not apply \cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch} and a statement of the boundary.
\end{itemize}
