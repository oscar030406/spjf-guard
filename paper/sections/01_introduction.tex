\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Before a common deadline, one submission that looped until its resource limit took a grader out of service and held up everything queued behind it. The maintainers of a university autograder found this when they replayed their busiest four hours \cite{peveler2019sandbox}. A contest judging system documents the same problem from the other side: its manual recommends one judging host per 20 teams and warns that the waiting time grows with fewer hosts \cite{domjudge_manual}. Both are shared execution services. Such a service runs jobs for many users on a fixed pool of $k$ servers, and a job that has started is not interrupted: it runs until it finishes or until it reaches a per-job time limit $L$. Serverless function platforms, build and test farms for continuous integration, batch inference queues and the judging back ends of programming-assignment platforms are all organised this way \cite{wasik2018ojsurvey}.

Two properties of the load make such a service slow down exactly when it is used most. Arrivals follow an external calendar, so they are neither stationary nor independent; the same crowding before a fixed cutoff has been measured directly \cite{konig2005deadline,alfi2007deadline,alfi2009deadline}. And a small fraction of the jobs carries most of the work. On the serverless invocation trace we use \cite{azure2021trace,zhang2021harvested}, the longest \devnum{1\%} of invocations account for \devnum{44\%} of the total work. On the main judging trace, after the analysis service cap is applied, the longest \devnum{1\%} still account for \devnum{37.5\%}, and for \devnum{27.5\%} on the second. A few very long jobs therefore sit in front of many short ones when the queue is longest.

The mechanism we exploit is visible in a queue of three jobs on one server (Figure~\ref{fig:threejob}). Job $A$ loops forever and is stopped by the 60-second limit; jobs $B$ and $C$ take 0.2 seconds each. In arrival order the mean wait is 40.1 seconds. Shortest first, it is 0.2 seconds, at a cost of 0.4 seconds to $A$. The server does the same total work in both orders. Nothing has been simulated yet. The three numbers are arithmetic, and they rest on one assumption: that the scheduler knows, before $A$ starts, that $A$ will be long. That assumption raises three questions, and the paper is organised around them. Can the cost of a job be estimated when it arrives, from history that is visible at that instant? Given estimates, what should a non-preemptive scheduler rank by? And what happens to one job when the estimates are wrong?

The third question is the one an operator asks first, because it is the one a student will ask. Ordering by prediction improves the bulk of the distribution and can damage the tail: in our busiest configuration one job waited almost a hundred job-limits longer than it would have under first-come first-served (Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}). The scheduling-with-predictions literature bounds an expectation against an optimal or shortest-remaining-processing-time reference, and its constant-factor results assume that the prediction error is bounded multiplicatively \cite{scully2022uniform,mitzenmacher2025queueing}. No one can promise that about a deployed predictor. There are results on single axes. One line bounds the maximum stretch over all jobs on a preemptive machine, which is not an expectation \cite{zhao2025fairpredictions}. Another bounds a request's latency in a stationary light-tailed queue against the workload the system holds at the request's boosted arrival instant, using the work that overtakes it \cite{yu2024boost,yu2025twotraffics}; read on a sample path, that is close to a bound against first-come first-served, but it is not the form those papers state. What we do not find is the combination: a first-come first-served reference, stated per job, on an arbitrary arrival sequence with arbitrary predictions, without preemption, on $k$ servers, and enforced online around whatever policy is underneath rather than analysed for one fixed policy.

We change the reference point instead of strengthening the assumption. For each job separately, we bound how much longer it waits than it would have waited under first-come first-served on the same arrival sequence, assuming nothing about the arrival process and nothing about prediction quality. The mechanism is an overtake budget. For every waiting job we account the total work of later-ordered jobs that completed while it waited, and at each dispatch decision the earliest-ranked waiting job whose account has reached its budget takes precedence. The guard wraps an arbitrary base scheduler, including a learned one, and touches only the dispatch decision. One parameter matters for the promise, a cap $B_{\max}$ on the budget. What the budget does below that cap is a design freedom: it changes how much of the base policy's benefit survives and which jobs bear the harm, and it changes the promise not at all.

We budget work rather than count overtakes because an identity puts the guarantee in units of work. A job's delay relative to first-come first-served equals its net overtaken work divided by $k$, to within $(2-2/k)L$, for every non-preemptive work-conserving $k$-server policy and every arrival sequence. The identity does not refer to the policy, and it holds in both directions, which is what makes a work budget the right unit and a bound on the \emph{number} of overtakes the wrong one. The guarantee is measured in units of $L$, so it is most informative when $L$ is comparable to the waits an operator cares about. A computing farm whose jobs run for days is a case where the certificate is too coarse for those waits; that does not rule out tighter guarantees for a restricted policy or workload.

On the first two questions, arrival-time history is enough to tell the heavy jobs from the rest on all three platforms. We compare three families of predictor under one split and one visibility protocol: tabular models \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, a sequence model over each user's history, and a heterogeneous graph neural network over the user, task and assignment entities \cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn}. The graph model does not improve on the tabular one. Removing every edge leaves its classification scores where they were, and so does permuting the edges at random, so the signal is in each entity's own history rather than in the relational structure. The edges help only where that history is missing: on tasks with fewer than five prior results the graph model's rank correlation exceeds the tabular model's by \devnum{0.146}.

We rank by the estimated conditional expected cost $\mathbb{E}[C \mid x]$ rather than by a log-scale prediction, whose back-transform generally differs from the arithmetic mean; the single-server interchange argument motivates that choice without establishing optimality for several servers, arbitrary arrivals or deadline-window percentiles. Errors in the fitted score are asymmetric: treating a heavy job as light, and letting it run first, is the expensive mistake by about an order of magnitude (Sections~\ref{sec:scheduling} and~\ref{sec:exp_rank}).

We make three contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item We prove the net-overtake identity, show that its constant $(2-2/k)L$ is an exact supremum, approached and never attained, and prove the corollary in the other direction: any per-job guarantee of this kind bounds the overtaken work, up to an additive $O(kL)$.
  \item We give an overtake-budget guard that wraps any base scheduler and bounds every job's wait against first-come first-served, for $k$ servers, arbitrary arrivals and arbitrary predictions. The bound reads the budget only through its cap, so the operator sets $B_{\max} = k(G - (3-2/k)L)$ from a promise $G$ in seconds, with no data. The additive constant $(3-2/k)L$ is an exact supremum, never attained, and it grows with $k$ from $L$ towards $3L$. One budget shape, whose allowance grows with the wait a job has already had, carries a multiplicative bound as well. The maximum waiting time that deployed schedulers use makes a promise of the same form, and we show what it gives up: near saturation it charges a job for the backlog ahead of it. From the arrival log alone we also bound the absolute wait, so an operator can promise a number of seconds rather than a margin over arrival order. We state which assumptions can be dropped and show by counterexample that work conservation cannot.
  \item We evaluate on real logs from two programming-assignment judging platforms \cite{codebench_dataset,accoding2024} and a serverless invocation trace \cite{azure2021trace}, with every simulated job checked against the bound, and we compare cost predictors on causally visible history, with two conclusions that carry into the scheduler: expected-cost ordering performs better than the log-scale alternative in the reported source-clock comparisons, and the fitted score is more sensitive to severe underestimates. Two applicability conditions, read off six workloads \cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch,taskcluster_docs,treeherder_docs}, say where the method does not apply, and most of the six fail one of them.
\end{itemize}

Three qualifications hold over everything reported below. The judging logs record execution demand and do not identify a historical shared queue, so the pooled capacity and the replayed waiting times are constructions of ours (Section~\ref{sec:data}); a capacity sweep, a timed-work service, a two-worker deployment on a course platform and a closed-loop replay test how far the results depend on that construction (Sections~\ref{sec:exp_physical} and~\ref{sec:limits}). Every figure in this paper is a development figure: the sealed parts of three traces, the main one's three terms among them, will be opened once, after the method, the parameters and the primary metric have been frozen, and their numbers reported separately (Section~\ref{sec:sealed}). And the empirical headline uses scores that use only results available in the replay: each policy's replay is repeated, leaving out every result that was not yet complete when the job that uses it arrived, until no score uses an unavailable result. Under that protocol Guard($\devnum{600}$) closes $\devnum{0.672}$ of the gap between first-come first-served and shortest-job-first at the busiest load, with interval $\devnum{[0.602, 0.743]}$, against $\devnum{0.801}$ under the source platform's own result clock, which we keep as an optimistic reference (Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}).
