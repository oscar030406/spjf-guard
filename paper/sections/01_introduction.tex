\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many computing services let a large number of users share a fixed set of machines. A
university system that compiles and tests every programming assignment its students
submit works this way \cite{wasik2018ojsurvey}; so does a service that runs a software
project's tests each time a developer changes the code, known as continuous integration
(CI), and a cloud platform that runs small user functions on request. Each submission
becomes a job, and the jobs wait in one queue for $k$ identical machines, which we call
servers. A server that becomes free takes the next job and runs it without interruption,
until the job finishes or reaches a time limit $L$ that the service imposes on every job,
and then reports the completion to the scheduler over the network.

Two features of the demand make such a service slowest exactly when it is busiest.
Submissions follow external calendars, such as a coursework deadline or a release date,
so they arrive in bursts, and the crowding before a fixed cutoff has been measured
directly \cite{konig2005deadline,alfi2007deadline,alfi2009deadline}. A small fraction of
the jobs also carries most of the work. In the log of cloud-function calls we use
\cite{azure2021trace,zhang2021harvested}, the longest \devnum{1\%} of the calls account for
\devnum{44\%} of the total running time. In the two logs of students' programming
submissions, with each running time capped at that platform's time limit, the longest
\devnum{1\%} account for \devnum{37.5\%} and \devnum{27.5\%}. The university grading system shows both
features at their sharpest, and it is our first case. Before a common deadline,
submissions that looped until their time limit occupied the graders, and the waits of
everything queued behind them grew in a cascade \cite{peveler2019sandbox}. The manual of a
programming-contest judging system recommends one judging machine per 20 teams and warns
that waits grow with fewer machines \cite{domjudge_manual}.

Ordering the queue by predicted cost exploits the second property, as a queue of three
jobs on one server shows (Figure~\ref{fig:threejob}). Job $A$ loops and is stopped by the
60-second limit; jobs $B$ and $C$ take 0.2 seconds each. In arrival order the mean wait is
40.1 seconds; shortest first, it is 0.2 seconds, at a cost of 0.4 seconds to $A$. These
numbers are arithmetic and rest on one assumption, that the scheduler knows before $A$
starts that $A$ will be long. When an estimate is wrong, the same ordering can hold a
job back for a long time. In our simulation of the busiest period we tested, driven by
the recorded submissions, one job waited almost a hundred time limits longer than it
would have under first-come first-served (FCFS) order (Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}).

A line of work on algorithms with predictions bounds how an algorithm performs as a
function of the error of the predictions it is given
\cite{lykouris2021caching,purohit2018predictions}. For scheduling, these bounds compare
the total or mean completion time with that of an optimal schedule, and the constant-factor
results assume that the prediction error is bounded
\cite{im2021nonclairvoyant,lattanzi2020learnedweights,lindermayr2022permutation,scully2022uniform,mitzenmacher2025queueing}.
A predictor in service carries no such bound, and an operator is asked about individual
jobs rather than averages. Two results come closer: one bounds the worst slowdown of any
job on a machine that may interrupt jobs \cite{zhao2025fairpredictions}, and one bounds a
request's delay in a queue with steady, light-tailed demand \cite{yu2024boost,yu2025twotraffics}.
We have not found a guarantee that is stated for every job against FCFS, holds for any
arrival sequence and any predictions, allows no interruption, covers $k$ servers, and can
be enforced while any scheduling rule runs underneath. This paper answers three
questions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Can a job's running time be estimated when it arrives, from the history available
  at that moment?
  \item How should a scheduler that cannot interrupt jobs order the queue once it has
  such estimates?
  \item What can the service promise every job when the estimates are wrong, and when the
  completion reports it relies on arrive late?
\end{enumerate}

For the third question we change the reference point instead of assuming more about the
predictions. For each job, we bound how much longer it waits than it would have under
FCFS on the same sequence of submissions, with no assumption about the arrival process or
about prediction quality. The mechanism is an overtake budget. For every waiting job the
scheduler adds up the running time of the jobs that arrived later but were started ahead
of it, and as soon as that total reaches the job's budget, the job is served before any
later arrival. This guard sits on top of any scheduling rule, including one driven by a
learned predictor, and changes only which job starts next; a cap $B_{\max}$ on the budget
fixes the promise. The budget counts running time rather than the number of jobs that
overtake, because of an identity we prove: under any scheduling rule that never leaves a
server idle while jobs wait and never interrupts a job, a job's extra wait relative to
FCFS equals the net running time of the jobs that overtook it, divided by $k$, up to an
error of at most $(2-2/k)L$. The guard learns of completions from the servers' reports, so
we also bound what it promises when these reports cross a network and arrive late.

For the first two questions we compare three kinds of predictor, trained and tested on
the same data under the same rule about what may be seen when a job arrives:
gradient-boosted decision trees \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, a gated recurrent unit (GRU) that
reads each user's earlier jobs, and a heterogeneous graph neural network (GNN) over users,
programming tasks and assignments, of the relational graph convolutional network (R-GCN)
type \cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn}. The history available at arrival separates the long
jobs from the short ones on all three platforms we test it on. The graph network does not
improve on the trees on average, and its edges add only \devnum{0.006} in Spearman
correlation over all test submissions, so most of the information lies in each user's and
each task's own history.
On tasks with fewer than five earlier results the graph network does rank jobs better,
by \devnum{0.146} in Spearman correlation over the trees and by \devnum{0.028} over the
same network with shuffled edges (Section~\ref{sec:prediction}). The scheduler orders jobs by their expected running time
given the observed features, and for our fitted predictor, treating a long job as
short costs about ten times more than treating a short job as long
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_rank}).

This study makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We prove the identity above and show that its error constant $(2-2/k)L$ cannot be
  improved. Conversely, any per-job promise of this kind limits the running time of the
  jobs that overtake a job, up to an additive $O(kL)$, whereas a limit on how many jobs
  overtake it is not required and, on our main log, is looser by a factor of
  \devnum{150}.
  \item We give the guard and prove that it keeps every job's extra wait over FCFS below
  $B_{\max}/k + (3-2/k)L$, for $k$ servers, any arrivals and any predictions; the constant
  cannot be improved. An operator who wants to promise $G$ seconds sets
  $B_{\max} = k(G - (3-2/k)L)$ and needs no data to do so. When completion reports arrive
  up to $\delta$ late, the promise grows by $L + \delta$, and it does not grow at all if
  each server reports its own completion before taking its next job. We also bound the
  absolute wait from the arrival record alone, and compare the guard with a maximum
  waiting time, which makes a promise of the same form.
  \item We evaluate the method on six logs: two from university systems that grade
  students' programming submissions \cite{codebench_dataset,accoding2024}, one from a
  cloud-function platform \cite{azure2021trace}, two from CI machine pools
  \cite{taskcluster_docs,treeherder_docs} and one from an industrial compute farm
  \cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch}. Every simulated job is checked against the
  proved bound. At the busiest load, with every score computed from the results
  available when its job arrives, a guard that promises 600 seconds keeps
  $\devnum{0.705}$ (bootstrap interval $\devnum{[0.648, 0.768]}$) of the reduction in
  the 99th-percentile wait before deadlines that shortest job first (SJF), which knows
  every running time in advance, achieves over FCFS (Section~\ref{sec:exp_visibility}).
\end{enumerate}

Two qualifications apply throughout. The grading logs record how long each submission
ran but not how long it waited, so the shared queue in our simulations is our own
construction (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Most numbers reported come from data we used while
developing the method. Three academic terms of the main log were held back unread and
opened once, after the method and its parameters were frozen. On them the guard keeps
\sealednum{0.894} of the reduction at peak load, against \devnum{0.705} on the development
terms (Section~\ref{sec:exp_sealed}).
