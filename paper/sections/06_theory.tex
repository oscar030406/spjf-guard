\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

An operator who puts a prediction-driven scheduler in front of students wants to know
the worst that can happen to one job, not what happens on average. Everything in this
section is therefore stated for one fixed run: one arrival sequence, one vector of
service times, one realisation of whatever randomness the base policy uses. We call
such a statement pathwise. No stationarity, no independence and no assumption about
score quality enters a proof, and the upper bounds hold even against a base policy
that knows the future. The section has three parts. An identity ties a job's delay
relative to first-come first-served to the work that overtook it; two bounds turn that
identity into the guarantee of Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}; and a set of constructions
shows that each constant in those bounds is an exact supremum, so that no smaller
constant would do. Every statement kept here comes with the idea of its argument;
Supplementary Sections~S4 and~S8 give every deferred proof, every extremal
construction and the statements this section only summarises, S8 opening with the idea
of each construction beside it.

\subsection{Conventions}

We use the model of Section~\ref{sec:model} throughout, and fix three readings
the proofs depend on. The dispatches at one instant form a \emph{phase}, ordered
as the policy performs them, and the dispatch sequence behind $j \prec i$ is the
concatenation of the phases; first-come first-served dispatches in increasing
rank inside a phase. A job dispatched in the same phase as $i$ executes no work
during $[a_i, s^P_i)$, that interval ending at the instant of the phase, yet
contributes its whole $C_j$ to $\mathrm{In}_i$. Remaining work at $a_i$ is read
\emph{after} the arrivals at $a_i$, so a job of rank below $i$ arriving at
exactly $a_i$ counts with its full service time.

\subsection{The Workload Gap between Two Policies}

Write $U_Q(t)$ for the unfinished work at time $t$ under policy $Q$, counting
every job with $a_j \le t$, and $N_Q(t)$ for the number of jobs present. The
unfinished-work vector of a $k$-server queue obeys the Kiefer--Wolfowitz
recursion~\cite{kiefer1955queues}, and first-come first-served minimises that
vector in the weak-majorisation order for systems in which arrivals are
allocated to servers in a manner that does not depend on their service
times~\cite{daley1987fcfs}. The policies compared below are not restricted that
way, so what carries the
single-server argument to $k > 1$ here is a two-sided bound between two arbitrary
policies rather than an ordering.

\begin{lemma}[Workload comparison]
\label{lem:gap}
Let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be non-preemptive work-conserving $k$-server policies run on
the same input. Then $|U_{Q_1}(t) - U_{Q_2}(t)| \le (k-1)L$ for every $t$. For
$k \ge 2$ the inequality is strict, $|U_{Q_1}(t) - U_{Q_2}(t)| < (k-1)L$, and
the constant is the exact supremum, approached but never attained. At $k = 1$
both sides vanish: one server executes work at rate $1$ whenever at least one job
is present, so every work-conserving policy has the same unfinished work at every
instant.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
The difference $D = U_{Q_1} - U_{Q_2}$ is continuous and piecewise linear. At
every instant at which $U_{Q_1}$ exceeds $(k-1)L$ it cannot increase, since more
than $k-1$ jobs are then present under $Q_1$ and every server of $Q_1$ is busy.
Tracking $D$ back to the last instant at which $U_{Q_1}$ was below the threshold
gives the bound. The proof, the strictness, and the cascade that shows the
constant cannot be lowered are in Supplementary Sections~S4.1 and~S8.1.
\end{proof}

Two work-conserving policies can therefore drift apart in unfinished work, but
only by what $k-1$ servers can hold; at one server they never drift at all.

\subsection{The Net-Overtake Identity}

For a job $i$ and a policy $Q$, let $R^Q_i$ be the remaining work at time $a_i$,
under $Q$, of the jobs of rank below $i$, read after the arrivals at $a_i$; and
let $\rho^Q_i$ be the remaining work at $s^Q_i$ of the jobs dispatched strictly
before $i$ in $Q$'s dispatch sequence that are still in service then. Write
\[
  \Delta_i \;=\; k\bigl(W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i]\bigr)
              \;-\; \bigl(\mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i\bigr).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Net-overtake identity]
\label{thm:identity}
For every non-preemptive work-conserving $k$-server policy $P$, every input and
every job $i$,
\[
  \Delta_i \;=\; \bigl(R^P_i - R^{\mathrm{FCFS}}_i\bigr) - \rho^P_i
                 + \rho^{\mathrm{FCFS}}_i ,
  \qquad
  \bigl|\Delta_i\bigr| \;\le\; 2(k-1)L ,
\]
with strict inequality for $k \ge 2$; equivalently
$\bigl|\,W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] - (\mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i)/k\,\bigr|
 \le (2 - 2/k)L$. At $k = 1$ the error term vanishes and
$W_P[i] - W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] = \mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i$ exactly.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Job $i$ waits throughout $[a_i, s^P_i)$, so every server is busy there and exactly
$k\,W_P[i]$ units of work are executed, none of it on $i$. Splitting that work by rank
gives the busy-period accounting
\begin{equation}
  k\,W_P[i] \;=\; R^P_i + \mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i - \rho^P_i ,
  \qquad \rho^P_i := \rho^{\mathrm{old}} + \rho^{\mathrm{new}} ,
  \label{eq:busy}
\end{equation}
where $\rho^{\mathrm{old}}$ and $\rho^{\mathrm{new}}$ are the work still unfinished at
$s^P_i$ of, respectively, the jobs dispatched strictly before $i$ and the overtakers,
and a same-phase overtaker contributes $C_j$ to both $\mathrm{In}_i$ and
$\rho^{\mathrm{new}}$ and nothing to the executed work. The jobs counted in $\rho^P_i$
occupy distinct servers and none of them occupies the server that takes $i$, so
$0 \le \rho^P_i \le (k-1)L$. Under first-come first-served
$\mathrm{In}_i = \mathrm{Out}_i = 0$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:gap} applied at $a_i$ bounds
$|R^P_i - R^{\mathrm{FCFS}}_i|$ by $(k-1)L$, strictly for $k \ge 2$; subtracting the two
instances of \eqref{eq:busy} gives the identity. Each direction uses only two of the
three ranges, which is why the slack is $2(k-1)L$ and not $3(k-1)L$, and at $k = 1$ all
three terms vanish. Supplementary Section~S4.3 gives the proof in full.
\end{proof}

Read from right to left, the identity turns a bound on overtaken work into a
bound on delay, with nothing else assumed about the policy.

\begin{corollary}[A work budget is sufficient]
\label{cor:sufficient}
If a policy keeps $\mathrm{In}_i - \mathrm{Out}_i \le Z$ for every job, in
particular if it keeps $\mathrm{In}_i \le Z$, then
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le Z/k + (2 - 2/k)L$ for every job.
\end{corollary}

A scheduler that never lets more than $Z$ units of work pass a waiting job
therefore holds every wait within $Z/k + (2-2/k)L$ of arrival order, whatever it
ranks by. That is the whole mechanism of Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}.

\subsection{The Constant \texorpdfstring{$2(k-1)L$}{2(k-1)L} Is Exact}

The error term of Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} is not an artefact of the proof, although
neither search nor real input reaches it: the extremal instances are geometric cascades
needing hundreds of jobs, which nothing in a replayed workload arranges.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_bound} and Supplementary Section~S3.13 report how far below the
constant the measured residual stays, on small instances and on the trace alike.

\begin{theorem}[Tightness of Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}]
\label{thm:tight1}
For every fixed $k \ge 2$, service bound $L>0$, and $\varepsilon > 0$ there are
inputs, work-conserving policies $P$ and jobs $i$ with
$\Delta_i > 2(k-1)L - \varepsilon$, and there are others with
$\Delta_i < -2(k-1)L + \varepsilon$. Hence
$\sup |\Delta_i| = 2(k-1)L$, a supremum that is never attained.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Both witnesses start from the cascade of Supplementary Section~S8.1, which drives
$U_P - U_{\mathrm{FCFS}}$ to $(k-1)L\,(1 - ((k-1)/k)^m)$ in $m$ rounds. The
upward witness then injects $k-1$ long jobs, the victim, and a stream of unit
jobs the base policy prefers, so that first-come first-served starts the victim
at once with $\rho^{\mathrm{FCFS}}_i = (k-1)L$ while $P$ holds it until the
stream runs out; the downward witness is the mirror image, with $P$ dispatching
$k-1$ jobs of service $L$ in the victim's own phase. The construction is first
given at scale $\widehat L = k^m$, and every arrival time and service time is
then multiplied by $L/\widehat L$, so the limiting sequence keeps the stated $L$
fixed. Supplementary Section~S8.2 gives both witnesses in full, with the exact
values they attain. Non-attainment follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}.
\end{proof}

The two directions do not have the same content. Upward, the extremal value is a
statement about delay. Downward, half of it is the bookkeeping convention that
charges an overtaker dispatched in the victim's own phase, which executes nothing
while the victim waits. Supplementary Section~S4.3 gives the remark in full, with
the same-phase shares, the executed-work variant under which the upward supremum
is unchanged and the best downward witness falls to $(k-1)L$, and the two reasons
we keep the dispatch-sequence convention.

\subsection{Necessity, and What a Count of Overtakes Costs}

Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} has a converse. A policy that promises anything
about individual jobs is forced to bound overtaken work.

\begin{lemma}[Outbound work under a promise]
\label{lem:outbound}
Let $i$ be a job, and suppose $\mathrm{excess}_P[i] \le G$ and
$\mathrm{excess}_P[j] \le G$ for every job $j$ counted in $\mathrm{Out}_i$. Then
$\mathrm{Out}_i \le k\,(G - \mathrm{excess}_P[i] + L)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Every job counted in $\mathrm{Out}_i$ starts inside a window of length
$G - \mathrm{excess}_P[i]$, and of the jobs one server starts inside such a
window all but the last complete inside it. Supplementary Section~S4.7 gives the
proof.
\end{proof}

A promise therefore limits not only the work that may pass a job but the work
that job may itself pass. That second limit is what makes the converse work.

\begin{corollary}[A work budget is necessary]
\label{cor:necessary}
If $P$ satisfies $\mathrm{excess}_P[j] \le G$ for every job on some input, then
$\mathrm{In}_i \le kG + (3k-2)L$ for every job $i$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Add Lemma~\ref{lem:outbound} to Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}, where the terms in
$\mathrm{excess}_P[i]$ cancel. Supplementary Section~S4.7 gives both proofs.
\end{proof}

Together the two corollaries say that bounding $\mathrm{In}_i$ and offering a
per-job guarantee are the same requirement, up to an additive $O(kL)$ in the
budget. Neither says that Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} is the least restrictive
rule with its guarantee, and we do not claim it.

Bounded-overtaking mechanisms in the literature limit instead how many times a job may
be passed~\cite{grosof2021nudge,vanhoudt2022nudgek,grosof2022wcfs}. Inside the model of
Section~\ref{sec:model} such a count is not necessary for a per-job guarantee, and it
gives one only at the worst-case charge of $L$ per pass. That charge makes it loose
against a work budget by the ratio $L/\bar{C}$, \devnum{one to two orders of magnitude}
on our traces. Supplementary Section~S4.4 states this as a remark and proves both
halves, and it carries the conversion from a count to a promise that the Skip baseline
of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} uses.

\subsection{The Guarantee of the Guard}

We now bound what Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} does. Nothing below restricts the
base policy, which may be randomised, learned, or clairvoyant. Nothing restricts
the budget rule either, beyond the cap: it may depend on the job, may decrease
over time, and may take different values at two dispatches made at the same
instant.

\begin{theorem}[Guard bound]
\label{thm:guard}
Fix a base policy $A$, a cap $B_{\max} \ge 0$, and a budget rule that respects
the cap, that is, a function $\mathrm{budget}(q,t)$ satisfying
$0 \le \mathrm{budget}(q,t) \le B_{\max}$ for every waiting job $q$ and every
dispatch epoch $t$; run Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} with that rule. For every
input and every job $i$, writing $W = W_P[i]$ for the wait under the wrapper,
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{In}_i &\;<\; B_{\max} + kL ,
  \label{eq:guardin}\\
  W &\;<\; W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] + B_{\max}/k + (3 - 2/k)L ,
  \label{eq:guardadd}
\end{align}
where \eqref{eq:guardin} is asserted whenever $i$ has at least one overtaker.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Read the last overtaker of $i$: at the instant it is dispatched, $i$ is
waiting and is not in the fired set, so $\mathrm{over}[i]$ is below its budget there.
The overtakers dispatched earlier and not yet finished occupy at most $k-1$ distinct
servers, and the last one adds a further $L$, which is the $kL$ of
\eqref{eq:guardin}. Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} with $\mathrm{Out}_i \ge 0$ turns that
into \eqref{eq:guardadd}. Supplementary Section~S4.5 gives the proof in full.
\end{proof}

Nothing in the proof reads the budget rule except through the cap, so the shape
of the budget below the cap is a free choice and the promise is unaffected by
it. One shape carries a second bound, in which the allowance grows with the wait
the job has already had rather than being flat in it. For the age-relative budget
$\mathrm{budget}(q,t) = \min\bigl(B_0 + \eta k (t - a_q),\ B_{\max}\bigr)$, with one
constant $B_0 \ge 0$ for every job and $\eta \in [0,1)$, the wait obeys
$(1 - \eta)\,W < W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] + B_0/k + (3 - 2/k)L$; Supplementary
Section~S4.5 states that as a proposition and proves it. The same bound written with a
common $B_0$ is false for the queue-length shape of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling}, by a
margin that grows with $\gamma$, so for $\gamma > 0$ we claim Theorem~\ref{thm:guard}
alone and Supplementary Section~S4.6 gives the counterexample. The cap, and with it the
promise, is unaffected either way.

A scheduler that learns $C_j$ at completion cannot charge work in progress, so
only completed work is charged; the $kL$ in \eqref{eq:guardin} comes from that, and it
is what forces the additive term. Inverting \eqref{eq:guardadd} gives
the parameter rule of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling}:

\begin{corollary}[The cap that offers a promise]
\label{cor:params}
Setting $B_{\max} = k\bigl(G - (3 - 2/k)L\bigr)$ makes Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} offer
the service guarantee $G$, whatever the budget shape below the cap and whatever
the base policy $A$. The choice is available when $G \ge (3 - 2/k)L$, and gives a positive budget only when
the inequality is strict; at equality $B_{\max} = 0$ and the wrapper is first-come
first-served.
\end{corollary}

This is the line an operator uses: a promise in seconds goes in, a budget in
units of work comes out, and nothing about the base policy has to be known.

One assumption can be traded for a smaller constant. If the service enforces a per-job
run-time cap $\ell_j \le L$ known at arrival, charging it at dispatch and refunding it at
completion keeps $\mathrm{In}_i$ within the budget, so the $kL$ of \eqref{eq:guardin}
goes and the additive constant falls from $(3-2/k)L$ to $(2-2/k)L$, a drop of exactly
$L$, the promise at $k = 1$ becoming $\mathrm{excess} \le B$ exactly. The information
model is then not the completion-only one of Section~\ref{sec:scheduling} and the
mechanism is conservative
backfilling's \cite{mualem2001backfilling,lindsay2013backfilling}, so Supplementary
Section~S7.6 carries it, with the single-server maximality and the counterexample that
rules maximality out for $k \ge 2$.

Neither constant in Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} can be improved. Under a constant budget
$B$ the term $B/k$ is reached exactly, on a queue of $k$ jobs of service $L$ followed
by a stream of unit jobs. The additive constant $(3 - 2/k)L$ of \eqref{eq:guardadd} is
the exact supremum at every $k$, approached by a cascade that has to be run for enough
rounds. Both sources of slack, the constant of Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} and
the $kL$ of \eqref{eq:guardin}, are saturated on that one instance, where the excess is
real waiting time rather than bookkeeping. Supplementary Section~S8.3 states this as a
proposition, gives both constructions, and reports what a hill-climb over small
instances suggests instead, beside the size the witness actually needs.

A guard that never fires leaves the base policy alone, and the condition is checkable
on the base policy's own run, before the wrapper is placed around it. If
$\mathrm{over}[q](t)$ stays below $\mathrm{budget}(q,t)$ for every waiting job at every
dispatch epoch of that run, the wrapped run is that run, with the same dispatch
sequence and the same waits. Supplementary Section~S4.2 states this as a proposition,
with the two conditions under which a constant budget meets its hypothesis and the two
counterexamples that make consistency one-sided.

\subsection{A Clock Keeps the Same Promise}
\label{sec:clock}

Deployed schedulers guard against starvation with a maximum waiting time. Recent
inference servers promote a request once it has waited past a threshold, and state no
bound on the wait~\cite{wu2023fastserve,luo2025autellix}. In the simplest form, once the
oldest waiting job has waited $\theta$ it is served next, and until then the base
policy decides. Call this Timeout($\theta$), and read ``oldest'' as the waiting job of
minimum rank, so that ties in arrival time are broken by input order and not by the
base policy. The rule reads arrival times and a clock, and never a service time. It
carries the same kind of promise as the guard.

\begin{proposition}[Timeout bound]
\label{prop:clock}
Fix a base policy and $\theta \ge 0$. For every input and every job $i$ with at least
one overtaker, Timeout($\theta$) gives $\mathrm{In}_i < k(\theta + L)$, and every job
waits
\[
  W \;<\; W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] + \theta + (3 - 2/k)L .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
From $a_i + \theta$ on, the minimum-rank waiting job has waited at least $\theta$, so
every dispatch until $s_i$ serves a job of rank below $i$. Each overtaker of $i$
therefore starts before $a_i + \theta$ and ends no later than $L$ after its start. The
overtakers that share a server run one after another inside $[a_i, a_i + \theta + L)$,
and summing over the $k$ servers bounds $\mathrm{In}_i$. Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}
with $\mathrm{Out}_i \ge 0$ turns that into the bound on the wait. Supplementary
Section~S4.11 gives the proof in full.
\end{proof}

At $\theta = G - (3-2/k)L$ the clock makes the promise that the guard makes at
$B_{\max} = k\theta$. The two rules differ in what they charge a waiting job for. The
clock charges it for all the work the servers do while it waits, $k(t - a_q)$. The
guard charges it only for the completed work of jobs that arrived after it. The
difference is the work of older jobs, which first-come first-served would have done
anyway. Near saturation that work is large. A job that arrives behind a backlog is
charged for the backlog, the clock fires for most of the queue, and the schedule falls
back to arrival order at the loads where ordering by cost pays most.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard} measures the difference.

The two rules also combine. Serve the minimum-rank waiting job if it has waited
$\theta$, and otherwise run Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}. When a job is served ahead of a
waiting job $i$ of lower rank, $i$ is then outside the guard's fired set, which is all
Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} uses; and every overtaker of $i$ still starts before
$a_i + \theta$, which is all Proposition~\ref{prop:clock} uses. Both bounds hold at
once. The order matters: a rule that serves the guard's fired job first can pass over
an aged head when budgets differ between jobs, and it breaks the clock's bound for the
queue-length and age-relative shapes (Supplementary Section~S4.11 gives the
instances). The clock's half reads no completion report, so the combined rule keeps
$W < W_{\mathrm{FCFS}} + \theta + (3-2/k)L$ when reports arrive late or not at all.

\subsection{An Absolute Bound from the Arrival Log}
\label{sec:absolute}

A promise relative to first-come first-served is one a student cannot check, since
nobody runs the counterfactual queue. The arrival log alone gives an absolute one. Let
$V_i^-$ be the work that a single server of rate $k$, fed the same jobs, would still
hold at $a_i$ from the jobs of rank below $i$:
$V_1^- = 0$ and
$V_i^- = \max\bigl(0,\ V_{i-1}^- + C_{i-1} - k(a_i - a_{i-1})\bigr)$. The largest value
$\sigma = \max_i (V_i^- + C_i)$ is the depth of the smallest token bucket of rate $k$
that the arrivals, counted in work, conform to, which is the burst parameter of network
calculus~\cite{cruz1991calculus,leboudec2001network}.

\begin{proposition}[Absolute bound]
\label{prop:absolute}
For every non-preemptive work-conserving policy and every job $i$,
$k\,W[i] \le V_i^- + (k-1)L + \mathrm{In}_i$. Hence
$W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] \le (V_i^- + (k-1)L)/k$, and under Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} with
cap $B_{\max}$
\[
  W \;<\; \frac{V_i^- + B_{\max}}{k} + \Bigl(2 - \frac{1}{k}\Bigr)L
    \;\le\; \frac{\sigma + B_{\max}}{k} + \Bigl(2 - \frac{1}{k}\Bigr)L .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
The accounting \eqref{eq:busy} gives $k\,W[i] \le R_i + \mathrm{In}_i$. A
work-conserving $k$-server queue never holds more than $(k-1)L$ above the fluid
server. Whenever it does, all $k$ servers are busy, since otherwise the queue is empty
and at most $k-1$ jobs in service hold at most $(k-1)L$, and then it drains at the
fluid rate. So $R_i \le V_i^- + (k-1)L$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} bounds
$\mathrm{In}_i$. Supplementary Section~S4.11 gives the proof in full.
\end{proof}

The comparison with the fluid server is the argument Grosof, Scully and Harchol-Balter
use for multiserver shortest-remaining-processing-time, where it bounds the work of jobs
below a size cutoff by $kx$~\cite{grosof2018srpt}. Here it covers every non-preemptive
work-conserving policy and all the work, and the constant is $(k-1)L$.

At $k = 1$ the fluid server is the queue, $V_i^- = W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i]$, and the
proposition is Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} again. For $k \ge 2$ it is sharper than a bound
that goes through $W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}$, which would pay the $2(k-1)L$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} a second time. An operator who promises that no job waits
longer than $D$, over a period whose arrivals conform to a bucket of depth $\sigma$,
sets $B_{\max} = kD - \sigma - (2k-1)L$: the capacity over the deadline, less the
burst, less $2k-1$ job-limits. The clock obeys the same bound with $B_{\max}$ replaced by
$k\theta$. The comparison with a single fast server has a history of false sample-path
claims. Wolff~\cite{wolff1987work} showed that earlier sample-path orderings between a
multichannel queue and single-channel comparison systems fail. The statement here is not
an ordering. It is an additive envelope around the fluid server that uses the service
cap $L$, and it is proved directly. Section~\ref{sec:exp_bound} measures how close the
bound comes on the traces, and how well one period's $\sigma$ predicts the next.

\subsection{Completion Reports That Arrive Late}
\label{sec:late}

Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} assumes that a completion is charged before the next dispatch.
In a distributed service the workers report completions over a network, and a server
may pull its next job before its report has reached the counter. Suppose every charge
reaches the counter within $\delta$ of the completion, while servers still pull the
instant they free.

\begin{proposition}[Late charges]
\label{prop:late}
Under Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} with a shared counter and charges delayed by at most
$\delta$, every job $i$ with an overtaker has
$\mathrm{In}_i < B_{\max} + kL + k(L + \delta)$, and
\[
  W \;<\; W_{\mathrm{FCFS}}[i] + B_{\max}/k + (4 - 2/k)L + \delta .
\]
The extra $L + \delta$ is attained in the limit at $k = 1$ and $k = 2$. If each server
applies its own completion report in the same step as its next pull, and only other
servers' reports travel late, Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} holds unchanged for every
$\delta$, lost reports included.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
At the last overtaker's dispatch the charged part is below the budget, as before. The
uncharged overtakers are those in service, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:guard}, and those
that finished in the last $\delta$ and whose charge has not arrived. On one server the
latter are at most one job that started before that window and jobs that fit inside
it, so they hold less than $L + \delta$. When a server reports before it pulls, any
stale completion must belong to a server that finished at the dispatch instant itself
and has not yet pulled; that server holds no job in service, so the per-server
uncharged work is at most one job, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:guard}. Supplementary
Section~S4.12 gives both proofs and the instances that attain the extra term.
\end{proof}

At $k = 1$ any $\delta > 0$ already costs a whole $L$: the single server pulls its next
job at the instant it finishes one, before that job's charge can arrive. Pulling ahead
of the report, which is what prefetching workers do, therefore changes the promise, and
reporting on the pull removes the change. The combined rule of
Section~\ref{sec:clock} gives a second remedy that needs no protocol at all, since its
clock half keeps $W < W_{\mathrm{FCFS}} + \theta + (3-2/k)L$ for any delay.

\subsection{Which Assumptions Can Be Dropped}

Four of the standing assumptions can go, and one cannot. The global service
bound $L$ may be replaced by the prefix maximum of the service times seen so
far, provided it is read at the right instant, and the right instant is not the
same for every statement. Servers may run at different speeds, the budget then
being divided by total capacity rather than by the server count. The per-job
time limit is already inside the model, because $C_j$ counts executed work. A
fixed setup paid before every service is absorbed into the job. Work
conservation is the assumption that cannot go: if a server may idle while jobs
wait, the identity fails by exactly the forced idle time, and release
constraints are the same phenomenon. Supplementary Section~S4.10 states all five
as two propositions, with the sharp forms and the counterexample that fixes
where the prefix maximum is read.

\subsection{A Floor of Order \texorpdfstring{$L$}{L}, and What a Guarantee Costs}

No choice of budget removes the last $L$ of the additive constant. A job can be
displaced before anything at all has completed, and at that instant a wrapper
that charges completed work has nothing to charge.

\begin{proposition}[Impossibility]
\label{prop:floor}
Fix a budget rule that assigns a positive budget to every waiting job at the
first dispatch epoch. Then there are a base policy $A$ that observes a job's
service time only at its completion, and an input, such that
Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard} run around $A$ with that rule leaves some job with
$\mathrm{excess} \ge L$. Consequently no promise $G < L$ can be made by a
wrapper of this family that holds for every base policy, or
for one prediction-ranked base policy under arbitrary predictions, which is the
generality in which Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} is stated.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Put $k+1$ jobs of service $L$ into an empty system at $t = 0$: nothing has
completed, so no waiting job fires, the wrapper accepts the base policy's choices, and
the first choice beyond the first $k$ ranks pushes back a job that first-come
first-served starts at once, leaving it with excess $L$. For a base policy that ranks
by predictions, such as SPJF-E, the predictions in the input already force this and
$A$ need not be chosen. The hypothesis can be neither
dropped nor tightened: as a claim about policies the statement is false, since
first-come first-served itself has excess $0$ on every input, and a rule that is $0$ at
the first dispatch epoch and positive later falls to the same instance placed behind a
saturating prefix. Supplementary Section~S4.8 gives the proof and the case analysis
that makes those cases exhaustive for the budget of Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}, which
either makes the wrapper first-come first-served around every base policy or admits a
base policy and an input with excess at least $L$.
\end{proof}

At one server the floor is the whole story. With a constant budget $B>0$ the
bound $B+L$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:guard} is the exact supremum of the excess,
approached and not attained; at $B=0$ the wrapper is first-come first-served and
the excess is exactly zero. Supplementary Section~S8.4 gives the single-server
family that approaches $B+L$ without reaching it.

The guarantee also caps how much reordering can gain at all, and not only how much
it may cost a single job.

\begin{theorem}[Price of the guarantee]
\label{thm:price}
Take $m$ jobs of service $L$ and $n$ jobs of service $\sigma < L$, the long ones
ranked first, all arriving at $t = 0$, and let $P$ be a non-preemptive
work-conserving policy with $\mathrm{excess}_P[j] \le G$ for every job. Define
$D_0=mn(L-\sigma)/k$, $E=2(1-1/k)L(m+n)$ and
$A=(kG+(3k-2)L)/(n\sigma)$. If
$D_0>E$, equivalently
$mn(L-\sigma)>2(k-1)L(m+n)$, then
\[
  \frac{\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{P}}
       {\overline{W}_{\mathrm{FCFS}} - \overline{W}_{\mathrm{SJF}}}
  \;\le\; A+\varepsilon_+,
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_+=\frac{E(1+A)}{D_0-E}.
\]
For fixed $k,L,\sigma,G$, the finite-sample term is
$O((m+n)/(mn))$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is in Supplementary Section~S8.5, which counts inverted pairs in two ways
and so reads the pairwise-interchange argument behind Smith's rule~\cite{smith1956} on
an arbitrary arrival sequence. The right-hand side is capacity times
guarantee, divided by the work that wants to overtake; it is linear in $G$ and
saturates at $1$ when $kG$ reaches the total service of the impatient class.
Because it applies to every policy with the guarantee and not only to
Algorithm~\ref{alg:guard}, it describes the trade-off an operator faces rather
than a weakness of this wrapper. The additive $(3k-2)L$ in it is slack: it comes
from applying Corollary~\ref{cor:necessary} to each long job separately, where
the long jobs can be charged together, and at $k = 1$ the aggregate argument in the same
place removes it. There the achievable fraction is known exactly: it is
$\lfloor G/\sigma \rfloor / n$, capped at $1$, which Supplementary Section~S8.5 states
as its own proposition and checks exhaustively over \devnum{1161} parameter
settings.

\subsection{Achievability and Maximal Permission}

Two further results say how far the guarantee can be pushed, so that the
earlier ones are not read only as a robust wrapper beside a necessary budget.
On the two-size batch family of Theorem~\ref{thm:price}, the guard around exact
shortest-job-first closes a fraction of the first-come-first-served-to-SJF gap
that meets the upper bound of that theorem, up to $2(3k-2)L/(n\sigma)$ and two
finite-sample terms. At one server, with integer sizes and budgets, the guard
that fires at $\mathrm{over} \ge G-L+1$ permits every action a wrapper with the
promise $G$ could safely permit, in a class that sees ranks, completions,
revealed sizes and the base policy's proposals but never an unfinished size.
Neither result gives instance optimality on arbitrary inputs, and for $k \ge 2$
the exact optimum stays open. Supplementary Section~S8.6 states both, with the
definition of the wrapper class, the single-server frontier they imply and the
proofs; supplementary theory material records an unbounded instance-optimality
counterexample and the limits of instance-dependent refinements.

Every uniform constant above is on the scale of $L$. Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}
establishes such a floor for the wrapper family under arbitrary opaque base
proposals, not for all scheduling mechanisms. Empirically, usefulness depends on
size dispersion, score predictability and the arrival structure; the CI pools
are negative cases rather than evidence of a necessary distributional condition.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_cross} quantifies that boundary on the evaluated workloads.
