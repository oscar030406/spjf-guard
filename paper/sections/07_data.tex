\section{Workloads and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The Workloads}
\label{sec:fields}

We use six logs of real jobs and one log of student activity (Table~\ref{tab:traces}). Our
main log comes from CodeBench, the programming-course platform of the Universidade Federal do
Amazonas in Brazil, which records every run of a student's code together with its duration
\cite{codebench_dataset}. A second grading log comes from ACcoding, an online judge used in
university programming courses \cite{accoding2024}. ACcoding's public release has no
timestamps, only submission identifiers. Without timestamps, we keep the real order and
costs of its jobs and draw their arrival times, so its results test the ordering mechanism
but not waiting in seconds.

Four logs come from outside education. The Azure Functions trace of 2021 covers two weeks of
calls to functions that run on demand \cite{azure2021trace,zhang2021harvested}. Two pools of
test machines that Mozilla uses for the Firefox browser, which we call CI pools A and B,
recorded the waits we use to check our simulator \cite{taskcluster_docs,treeherder_docs}.
In Intel's Netbatch compute farm \cite{netbatch2012trace,shai2014netbatch}, jobs may run for
up to \devnum{5.7} days, and this farm is the case where our method does not apply.

Finally, the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD) records the daily activity
of students in online courses \cite{oulad2017}. Because it contains no jobs, we use it only
to check that activity crowds before deadlines outside our own grading logs.

Three features of the main log shape how we use it, and Supplementary Section~S2 gives the
full audit. Since the timestamp of a run is the moment its result was written, we
reconstruct the arrival as $a_i = t_i - C_i$ and treat the recorded stamp as
$\mathrm{done}_i$.

Stamps are whole seconds while durations are in microseconds, which lets a model read the
fractional part of the gap to the previous record and learn from it something it should
not know. Given only that part, a classifier separates heavy from light jobs with AUROC
\devnum{0.8091}. This AUROC falls to \devnum{0.4499}, which is chance, when each stamp is
jittered deterministically, and the forward models move by less than \devnum{0.008}.

No run after \devnum{2019-08-28} lasts longer than \devnum{60} s, although the platform
documents no timeout. Hence we take the periods after that date, cap each duration at the
observed ceiling, $C^{\mathrm{cap}} = \min(C, 60)$, and impose $L = \devnum{60}$ s in our
pool, as a shared service would (Supplementary Table~S3).

\subsection{A Shared Pool Built from the Log}
\label{sec:overlay}
\label{sec:source_platform}
\label{sec:load}

CodeBench runs each student's code in a separate container \cite{codebench_home}, so its
log records how long each run took but not a shared queue. Our experiments therefore
replace the containers with a pool of $k$ servers fed by the recorded runs, a queue of our
own construction.

Three things support this construction. A fixed pool of judging machines on one queue is a
standard deployment \cite{domjudge_manual,peveler2019sandbox}. On the same demand, one
container per student keeps \devnum{1{,}636} containers alive at the p99, and each of them
runs code for \devnum{0.12\%} of its lifetime, whereas a pool of \devnum{14} servers holds
the p99 of waits before deadlines under \devnum{1} s. Finally, the simulator reproduces
waits that someone else recorded. When fitted on one part of each CI log, it returns the
other part's mean wait, hourly profile and per-job order (Supplementary Section~S2.3). None
of this shows that the original platform ever queued, and we make no claim about waiting
there.

A single course in a single term does not load even one server, so we overlay several. From
the \devnum{40} class-terms of the development period, each holding the submissions of one
class in one academic term, we superimpose \devnum{44} copies. The copies are aligned by
weekday and hour, and each is shifted by a whole number of weeks drawn at random. Overlaying whole
terms keeps the daily and weekly cycles and each job's position relative to its own
deadline, which compressing the gaps between arrivals would not. In all,
\devnum{17{,}634{,}760} jobs arrive over \devnum{30} weeks, and \devnum{4{,}219{,}556} of
them arrive in the \devnum{24} hours before an assessment deadline.

Three loads share this trace and differ only in the number of servers. Four of the five
overlays, which differ only in the drawn week shifts, use $k = \devnum{8}$, \devnum{5} and
\devnum{4}. On the fifth, whose busy hours carry the least work, $k$ is \devnum{7},
\devnum{5} and \devnum{4}, so tables that average over overlays print $k = \devnum{7.8}$ at
the lightest load. Averaged over the five overlays, the utilisation of the busiest hours is
\devnum{0.502}, \devnum{0.783} and \devnum{0.979}, and we call the three loads $\rho =
\devnum{0.5}$, \devnum{0.8} and \devnum{1.0}. These loads are \devnum{0.529},
\devnum{0.847} and \devnum{1.059} on the first overlay alone.

The five overlays are one construction and not five platforms. For intervals we use
\devnum{95\%} percentile intervals from \devnum{2{,}000} resamples of whole weeks, drawn once
and shared by every policy so that differences between policies are paired. Such intervals
cover variation over time, not the choice of terms or courses.

Arrivals follow the course calendar. When the number of runs per class is predicted a week
ahead, the log-scale RMSE is \devnum{1.243} from recent counts alone, \devnum{1.159} from
calendar features alone and \devnum{1.112} from both. Adding the calendar thus changes the
error by \devnum{$-0.1307$ $[-0.1395, -0.1213]$}.

OULAD shows the same pattern outside our grading logs. In each of its nine course
presentations, the three days before an assessment carry \devnum{1.29} to \devnum{2.95}
times the activity of other days. With calendar features, a warning of peak activity seven
days ahead reaches an F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) of
\devnum{0.593}, against \devnum{0.000} for a moving average of the previous two weeks.

\subsection{Development Data, Sealed Data and Parameter Selection}
\label{sec:sealed}
\label{sec:exp_prov}

Three logs have a part that was sealed before the method was designed: three academic
terms of CodeBench (\devnum{2023-1}, \devnum{2023-2} and \devnum{2024-1}), the last
\devnum{20\%} of ACcoding's submission identifiers, and OULAD's \devnum{2014} year. Of the
\devnum{809{,}330} sealed ACcoding rows, only the count and the first identifier have been
read. No cost, prediction or scheduling result has been computed on any sealed part, and
every access is recorded in the repository.

For this paper we will open only the CodeBench terms, once, after the method, its
parameters and the primary metric are frozen. That evaluation is not reported here, so every
number in this paper is a development number.

A rule fixed before the grids were run chooses the guard's shape parameters. Each of the
three shapes has a grid of parameter points, and the rule keeps the points whose worst harm
over \devnum{15} validation cells is at most $G/2$. Among those it takes the one with the
largest worst-cell gap closed (Supplementary Section~S5). The validation cells are the five
overlays at three loads, built on development terms other than the last.

However, the guard family and a coarse grid were explored on the evaluation overlays before
this rule existed. The selection is therefore not held out from development, and we do not
call any figure out of sample. Supplementary Section~S5 records what the rule selected,
which results the released code reproduces, and how the choice moves when one validation
cell is dropped.
