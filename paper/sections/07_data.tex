\section{Traces and Workload Characterisation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The Traces}

Both judging platforms record how long each submission ran, and neither records what it
waited for. Hence the pool, its capacity and the queue are ours, and this section
says how we built them and what supports the construction. Two programming-assignment
judging platforms supply the demand traces, and four further workloads bound the claim
from outside that setting. Each trace's use depends on whether its arrival times are observed or reconstructed,
whether its queue wait is recorded, and
how long a job may run there (Table~\ref{tab:traces}).

Both CI pools and the compute farm carry a recorded queue wait. We validate the simulator
against the CI pools, whose capacity is a countable set of physical machines that changes
only on reimaging or quarantine. The second judging platform's released tables have no timestamp column, only a
submission id, so job order inside a group and job sizes are real while
every arrival instant is drawn. Over \devnum{469} groups, the correlation between a group's first id and its recorded start
time is \devnum{0.9995}, but in \devnum{207} of
\devnum{468} consecutive transitions the id ranges overlap, so the id is an ordering and
not a clock. Hence its results check that the ordering mechanism transfers, not a wait in
seconds.

\subsection{What the Fields of the Main Trace Mean}

Several fields of that trace needed interpreting before a queueing model could use them. Supplementary Section~S2 gives the audit that establishes the readings below,
together with the comparison against the release statistics and the two truncated archives.

On an execution block, the timestamp is the moment the result was written, not the
moment the code was received. We therefore take $\mathrm{done}_j$ to be the recorded
stamp and reconstruct the arrival as $a_i = t_i - C_i$, and we carry the opposite
reading through the pipeline as a sensitivity with its own features and models. Stamps
are recorded to the whole second while costs are recorded to the microsecond, and that
opens a leak. We close it by jittering each stamp deterministically. We found the leak larger than expected, since a classifier given only the fractional part of the gap to the
previous record separates heavy from light jobs with AUROC \devnum{0.8091} on the
whole-second clock. After the jitter it scores \devnum{0.4499}, which is chance. Across the two clocks, the forward models stay within \devnum{0.008} of AUROC of each
other, so
the repair costs nothing.

The observed regime has an upper ceiling of \devnum{60} s, which is not evidence of a documented enforced timeout. The last run to reach a longer time ends at
\devnum{2019-08-28}. Hence the periods before that date stay out of the primary pool, and
where used at all they carry the later limit, $C^{\mathrm{cap}} = \min(C, 60)$
(Supplementary Table~S3). Groups of runs by the same user that end in the same second
are \devnum{7.53\%} of blocks and \devnum{26.28\%} of capped work. They overlap and
stretch each other, so we neither delete them silently nor treat each as an
independent arrival. We rebuild the scheduling trace twice, once with each group
collapsed into one job and once with the groups removed (Supplementary Table~S15).

\subsection{What the Source Platform Identifies}

On the platform that produced the main trace, a separate container is created for each
student and runs their code~\cite{codebench_home}. Its logs do not identify the waiting times
of a shared judging queue. We use recorded durations and reconstructed start-time
proxies as inputs to an assumed shared pool, whose capacity, queue and service order are our design. Neither this construction nor the absence of shared-queue
fields establishes that all source-platform work experienced no waiting.

Three things support the design. First, a fixed pool of judging hosts on one queue is a
standard deployment. A widely used contest judging system recommends ``one
judgehost per 20 teams''~\cite{domjudge_manual} on dedicated machines, and the
autograder study of Section~\ref{sec:intro} runs a configurable number of concurrent
graders and measures the queue wait~\cite{peveler2019sandbox}. In addition, dedicated per-entity capacity is known to sit idle in workloads like this one, which two
measurements outside our own traces record~\cite{shahrad2020wild,verma2015borg}. Second comes a measurement on the same demand. Replayed under the dedicated
design, that demand keeps \devnum{1{,}636} containers alive at the 99th percentile,
each executing code for \devnum{0.12\%} of the time it is alive, and a shared pool
holds the 99th percentile of deadline-window waits under \devnum{1} s with \devnum{14}
servers. Third, the simulator reproduces waits somebody else recorded, since refitting an
effective capacity and a setup term on one chronological part of each
continuous-integration window returns the held-out mean wait, the per-hour profile and
the per-job ordering. We give all three in full in Supplementary Section~S2.3, together with what the two
external measurements do not license, the six session reconstructions, the
five chronological splits, the sharpness check, the one quantile that does not survive
and the circularity of the original whole-window fit. Because the pooled side is simulated, none of this supports a claim that the platform
ever queued.

\paragraph{Capacity and physical checks.}
In the supplementary capacity sweep, the complete offered stream is retained, the number
of servers varies, and the archived embedded Tweedie fit is used without refitting.
In the physical experiment we execute FCFS, SPJF-E and a guard on the complete selected
development window, with timed slot occupancy and recorded enqueue, dispatch and
completion events. Thus it tests an executing rule but does not transport submitted
programs to new hardware. The selected empty-start window is distinct from a full-trace replay, with a worse guarded p99 and a failed job-level
agreement with the ideal simulator (Section~\ref{sec:exp_physical}, Supplementary Section~S6).

\subsection{Building the Shared Pool}
\label{sec:overlay}

Because a single class-term does not load one server, we overlay several. The pool takes only the development terms in the observed \devnum{60} s regime,
\devnum{40} class-terms in all, and replicates them \devnum{44} times, each copy aligned by weekday and hour and shifted by a
whole number of weeks drawn per copy. Overlaying whole terms rather than compressing
inter-arrival times preserves the daily and weekly cycle and each job's position relative
to its own deadline. The result is
\devnum{17{,}634{,}760} jobs over \devnum{30} weeks and \devnum{208.8} days. All three load levels share that trace and differ only in the pool size. Four of the five overlays
take \devnum{$k = 8/5/4$} and the fifth takes \devnum{$7/5/4$}, its work per busy hour
being the lowest of the five. On the first overlay that gives busy-hour utilisations of
\devnum{0.529}, \devnum{0.847} and \devnum{1.059}, and \devnum{0.502}, \devnum{0.783} and
\devnum{0.979} averaged over the five. A
deadline window is the \devnum{24} hours before an assessment's own end, and
\devnum{4{,}219{,}556} jobs arrive inside one. Five overlays are week-shifts of the same
terms, so they are one construction and not five platforms, and our intervals resample whole weeks, covering time but not the choice of terms. A trace built
from the last development term alone is
not available this way, since it would need \devnum{190} copies of its \devnum{8}
class-terms to load a pool of four.

\subsection{What the Load Looks Like}

Arrivals follow the course calendar, and the calendar predicts them. Under
grouped-class cross-validation, predicting the number of executions per class
\devnum{168} hours ahead gives log-scale RMSE
\devnum{1.243} with lag features alone, \devnum{1.159} with calendar features
alone, and \devnum{1.112} with both. A paired bootstrap over class-week blocks
gives a combined-minus-lag-only difference of
\devnum{$-0.1307$ $[-0.1395, -0.1213]$}.
% Source: evidence/codebench/out_codebench.txt, P4 grouped-class-CV h=168
% (lgbm_lags, lgbm_cal_only, lgbm_lags_cal) and paired CV h=168 rows.
 Similarly, the supporting trace shows the same shape elsewhere. Across its nine course-presentations, the three days up to an assessment
due date carry \devnum{1.29} to \devnum{2.95} times the clicks of the other test days,
and a seven-day-ahead peak warning on distinct active students goes from F1
\devnum{0.000} under a moving-average baseline to \devnum{0.593} once calendar
features are added.
% Sources: evidence/oulad/out_load.txt:155-158 (due-window click ratios per
% course-presentation, 1.29 to 2.95) and :65, :68 (T3, h = 7, peak F1 0.000 for the
% mean-of-14-days baseline, 0.593 with calendar features).

Costs are concentrated in a small fraction of jobs on every workload except the CI
pools, which motivates the empirical cost-concentration screen (Supplementary
Table~S3, which also records where the analysis bound $L$ comes from on every trace).
Supplementary Section~S2 gives what the arrival and service fields of each trace are,
which of them are proxies, and how many jobs each analysis truncation touches. Thus the traces give a demand model and a replayed queue, and no historical measurement of
either.




\subsection{Sealed Data}
\label{sec:sealed}

Three of the traces carry a part that was sealed before the method was designed.
On the main trace it is three terms, \devnum{2023-1}, \devnum{2023-2} and
\devnum{2024-1}. On the second judging platform it is the last \devnum{20\%} of the
submission-id range, \devnum{809{,}330} rows, of which only the row count and the first
id have been read. On the supporting trace it is the \devnum{2014}
academic year. We will open only the main trace's sealed terms for this paper, once,
after freezing the method, its parameters and the primary metric. The frozen pipeline
covers those three terms and nothing else, while the sealed parts of the other two
traces stay shut and are not reported. The serverless trace,
the compute farm and the two continuous-integration pools have no sealed part,
so every figure we give for them is development data. No cost distribution,
prediction or scheduling result has been computed on any sealed part, and every
access to one is entered in a record in the repository. Every number in this
section and in Sections~\ref{sec:prediction} and~\ref{sec:scheduling} is a
development number. The sealed evaluation remains pending in this manuscript, and Section~\ref{sec:exp_prov} states the current output scope.
