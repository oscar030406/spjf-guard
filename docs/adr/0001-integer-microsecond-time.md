# 0001 The simulator's clock and accounting use integer microseconds

The clock of `prechecks/guard_variants/guardkern.py` is float64 seconds; only the budget algebra uses integer microseconds. Every development-phase number came from it. When rewriting it there were two choices: copy the float clock, or move the clock to integer microseconds as well.

We moved to integer microseconds. The reason is that the guard's trigger condition is a comparison at an equality boundary, `over[q] >= B0 + eta k (t - a_q)`, and under a float clock `t` is built up from a long chain of `t + s[j]` additions. On a 30-week trace `t` is of order 1.8e7 seconds, where the spacing of doubles is about 2e-9 seconds, and the error of repeated addition has no upper bound. The error itself is small, but it decides which job a dispatch goes to, and the dispatch order changes the wait of every later job. The paper claims an exact per-job guarantee, and a dispatch decided by a floating-point mantissa does not fit that claim. Under integer microseconds the simulation result depends only on the input, not on evaluation order, compiler or machine.

The cost is that every input must first be quantised to microseconds (`Trace.from_seconds` does this), and agreement with the old kernel becomes something that has to be verified instead of something true by definition.

The verification was done, and the result has two halves. `tests/test_sim_crosscheck_prechecks.py` compares job by job on random instances at k = 1, 2, 4, 7 (5,000 jobs, 30% with identical arrival times), and all are equal. `scripts/check_reproduction.py` compares job by job on the five real overlay traces with 17.63 million jobs × three load levels: FCFS is exactly equal in every cell, the other policies are exactly equal in 26 of 30 cells, and in the remaining 4 cells between 3 and 21,030 jobs have different waits (the largest is 0.12% of that cell). The differences occur only for the policies that order by score. FCFS dispatch does not depend on any boundary comparison, whereas score ordering must first establish "which jobs are waiting at this moment", and `a[i] <= t` can flip under the accumulated error of the float clock. In other words, this is exactly the kind of non-determinism this ADR is meant to remove, and it does occur on the real traces; it is not a hypothetical risk.

This explanation is not a guess; a discriminating experiment was run: shift one 300,000-job trace as a whole by a number of whole seconds and rerun it. Under integer arithmetic every wait must stay the same, because every comparison is an integer comparison; under a float clock, the further from the origin, the coarser the representable spacing. The result: this repository's kernel leaves every wait unchanged job by job under shifts of 10³, 10⁶, 10⁸ and 10¹⁰ seconds (difference 0), while `guardkern.py` differs from that invariant answer on 289,550 of the 300,000 jobs at a shift of 10⁸ seconds and on 299,876 at 10¹⁰ seconds. The clock of the real traces is of order 1.8e7 seconds, at the far left of this curve, so the deviation is small but not zero.

The cost is therefore explicit: the v3.1 tables cannot be replayed bit for bit; they can only be replayed bit for bit up to the "exact clock" version. Which version is right can be judged independently: this repository's kernel also passed job-by-job comparisons against the Lindley recursion, the Kiefer–Wolfowitz recursion, and an independently written O(n²) transcription of Algorithm 1 (`tests/test_sim_guard.py`), and those references all use exact arithmetic themselves. The size of the deviation is in `docs/repo_build_status.md`.
