# Closed-loop replay of the development overlays: what changes and what does not

Development data only. Model and edge treatments in `MODEL.md`; numbers regenerated into
`out/report_tables.md` by `make_tables.py`. Overlays 0-2, load levels 0-2, four policies,
two edge treatments, 108 cells.

## 1. What the three cited papers actually say

- **Schroeder, Wierman & Harchol-Balter, NSDI 2006, "Open versus closed: a cautionary
  tale".** The abstract distinguishes a closed model, in which new arrivals are triggered
  only by job completions followed by think time, from an open model in which they arrive
  independently of completions, and reports a vast difference in behaviour between the two
  in real-world settings, synthesised into eight guiding principles. Two bear directly on
  us. Principle (iv): open systems benefit significantly from scheduling with respect to
  response time, closed systems improve much less. Principle (v): scheduling only
  significantly improves response time in closed systems under moderate load and a high
  multiprogramming level, and even there the differences between policies are a factor of
  about 2.5 rather than orders of magnitude. Principle (vii) sets the boundary: a partly
  open system behaves like an open one when a session holds few requests (5 or fewer as a
  rule of thumb) and like a closed one when it holds many (10 or more).
- **Zakay & Feitelson, MASCOTS 2014, "Preserving user behavior characteristics in
  trace-based simulation of parallel job scheduling".** The abstract argues that replaying
  the exact recorded submission timestamps retains an unimportant attribute at the expense
  of a more important one, because in a real system those times depend on how users react
  to the performance of previous jobs, and that it is more important to preserve the
  logical structure of dependencies between jobs than the specific timestamps. Their
  construction extracts the dependencies from the trace together with user behaviour,
  expressed as sessions and as think times between the termination of one batch of jobs
  and the submission of the next. That is the construction this study implements.
- **Shmueli & Feitelson, IEEE TPDS 20(7), 2009, "On simulation and design of
  parallel-systems schedulers: are we doing the right thing?"** The abstract argues that
  open-system trace-driven simulation has pushed every scheduler towards packing jobs to
  optimise metrics only conjectured to correlate with user satisfaction, proposes
  site-level simulation in which the workload is generated by user models that interact
  with the system, and reports that a user-aware scheduler (CREASY) raises user
  productivity by up to 50 % while the conventional metrics may actually degrade. Their
  warning is therefore about the *metric*, not only about the arrival process.

Our trace supports the Zakay-Feitelson dependency replay with a session of one job. It
does not support the Shmueli-Feitelson productivity metric, and nothing below claims to
measure satisfaction or productivity.

## 2. The observational evidence: do these users wait for their results?

This is the only part of the study that is a measurement rather than a simulation. On
`primary_rep0` (identical on all five overlays, which are week-shifts of the same terms):
17,634,760 jobs, 68,640 user chains (1,560 real student-terms replicated 44 times), 257
submissions per chain on average, 17,566,120 jobs with a predecessor.

- **The offset is almost always non-negative.** delta_i >= 0 for **98.38 %** of
  consecutive pairs, so on the paper's header reading the next submission almost always
  follows the previous result. The remaining 1.62 % are submission-gated; they are
  concentrated in the groups of runs by one user ending in the same second that
  Section 7 already reports (7.53 % of blocks, 26.28 % of capped work).
- **The offset is long.** Quantiles of delta over all pairs, in seconds: p1 -0.22,
  p5 4.71, p10 11.72, p25 35.04, **median 123.51**, p75 509.31, p90 2,755.16, p95
  69,190.78, p99 691,127.59. Inside deadline windows (4,217,092 pairs, 97.17 %
  result-gated) it is shorter but of the same order: p10 8.22, p25 29.23, median 109.03,
  p75 432.95.
- **The offset does not scale with the previous job's cost.** Among result-gated pairs the
  Spearman correlation of delta on C_pred is **0.0146** (2,000,000 sampled pairs). In the
  two C_pred bins that hold 97.4 % of the jobs (0.1-0.2 s and 0.2-0.5 s) the median delta
  is 123.3 s and 129.7 s. The bins above 0.5 s hold 1.6 % of jobs, and there the
  result-gated share drops to 0.38-0.87 and the median delta falls to near zero - that is
  the co-ending-group artefact, not a cost effect. Full table in `out/delta_vs_cpred.csv`.

Read plainly: the median student waits about two minutes after a result before submitting
again, roughly 700 times the median job cost (0.18 s) and of the same order as the
deadline-window p99 wait the open-loop replay produces at the two heavier loads (113.8 s
and 268.5 s under FCFS). The feedback is real and worth modelling, it is not the dominant
time scale, and it carries no measurable dependence on how long the previous job ran.
**The manuscript's claim in Section 9 that "our logs carrying no session boundaries, no
think times and no resubmission semantics" is wrong on the second and third counts and
should be withdrawn.**

## 3. Validation of the simulator

The closed-loop kernel is a port of `spjf_guard.sim.kernel` in which arrivals are released
by the chain instead of read from a sorted array. With gating disabled it reproduces the
package **exactly**: maximum absolute difference **0 microseconds** on every job, for
FCFS, SJF, SPJF-E and Guard(600), at all three load levels of overlay 0
(`out/validation.json`). The open-loop rows of `out/closed_cells.csv` also reproduce
`outputs/dev_tables/main_cells.csv` to every printed digit on p99, harm and max excess.

Policy definitions: SPJF-E ranks by the stored `tweedie` score - the **original**-clock
expected-cost variant, the one `main_cells.csv` uses, not the conservative headline.
Guard(600) is the selected capped-budget policy, b0 = 120*k/4 s, eta = 0.75, gamma = 0,
Bmax = k(600 - (3 - 2/k)*60), on the same score, with the guard exactly as Algorithm 1
(completed-work budget, min-rank fired job).

## 5. Does the benefit grow, shrink or reverse, and why

**It does not reverse anywhere.** In all 9 cells and both edge treatments the ordering
FCFS > Guard(600) ~ SPJF-E > SJF on the deadline-window p99 is preserved, and the fraction
of the FCFS-to-SJF gap closed stays in 0.81-0.85 under gating against 0.73-0.92 without it.
What changes is the *scale*: every wait falls by a factor of 1.3 to 3.8.

Three mechanisms, all visible in Table B.

1. **Gating shaves the busiest hour.** A job cannot arrive until its predecessor has
   finished, so a queue that builds up holds back the submissions that would have deepened
   it. The realised busy-hour utilisation falls from 0.505 to 0.450 at level 0, 0.807 to
   0.745 at level 1 and 1.009 to 0.942 at level 2. Level 2, overloaded in the open replay,
   is no longer overloaded; that alone explains most of FCFS's fall from 268.5 s to 71.4 s.
2. **FCFS thins its own burst hardest.** The delay a policy imposes is the delay its users
   inherit. FCFS's mean drift is 440 s at level 2 against 172 s for SPJF-E and 142 s for
   SJF: the baseline's long waits push its own successor submissions out of the peak far
   more than a short-job-first policy's do. The reference against which the benefit is
   measured therefore improves more than the treatment does, which compresses the absolute
   gap - FCFS falls 3.8x at level 2, SPJF-E only 1.6x. This is the effect Schroeder et al.
   describe in principle (iv): the closed loop is self-limiting, so there is less for
   scheduling to fix.
3. **Short jobs' users come back sooner under SPJF-like policies.** The counter-effect is
   present and small: SPJF-E's drift (90.5 s at level 1) is *above* SJF's (77.6 s) and
   Guard's is above both (93.3 s), because the jobs these policies delay are the long ones
   whose users then return late, while the short jobs they accelerate return early and
   re-enter the same window. It is not enough to offset mechanism 2 - the net movement of
   the gap closed is within +/-0.12.

**The one clear qualitative change is in the price of the guarantee.** The guard fires
because a job has been overtaken by a budget's worth of work; under gating the queues are
shorter, so it fires 25-45x less often (7.52 % to 0.27 % queue-weighted at level 2). Its
cost against unguarded SPJF-E collapses with it: Guard(600) is 41.6 % worse than SPJF-E on
the open-loop p99 at level 2 and 3.7 % worse under gating (1.0 % to 0.4 % at level 0,
9.6 % to 1.3 % at level 1). The guard's *value* is unchanged - it still caps the worst
wait at 1,074 s where SPJF-E reaches 5,783 s and SJF 4,234 s. Starvation of long jobs is a
property of the ranking, not of the arrival process, and gating does not cure it.

For Section 9, replacing the second and third sentences of the "Open-loop replay"
paragraph:

> Our logs do, however, identify each submission's author and its position in that
> student's sequence, and under the header reading of Section 7 the offset between one
> result and the next submission is a recorded think time: it is non-negative for 98.4 %
> of consecutive pairs, has median 123.5 s, and is uncorrelated with the previous job's
> cost (Spearman 0.015). We therefore replay the trace a second time in the closed form of
> Zakay and Feitelson, releasing each submission delta_i after its predecessor's completion
> in the replay when delta_i >= 0 and preserving the recorded inter-submission gap
> otherwise. Across the development overlays the two readings agree in direction: the
> fraction of the FCFS-to-SJF deadline-window gap that Guard(600) closes is 0.734, 0.827
> and 0.800 at the three loads under open-loop replay and 0.825, 0.809 and 0.810 under
> gating.

## 7. What a sceptical referee would still attack

1. **The think times were recorded under zero queueing delay.** The source platform gave
   each student a dedicated container, so every delta in the trace was produced by a
   student who never waited. The replay assumes delta is invariant to the wait. If real
   students shorten their think time when a result arrives late (impatience) or lengthen it
   (disengagement), both the magnitude and possibly the sign of the effect change, and
   nothing in this trace can distinguish the two. This is the weakest joint in the study
   and should be stated as such.
2. **No abandonment and no content feedback.** A student who waits never quits, never
   switches exercise, and submits exactly the code they submitted in the log. Shmueli and
   Feitelson's point is that the metric that moves under feedback is productivity, and that
   a user-aware scheduler can improve it while conventional waiting metrics degrade. This
   study measures only the waiting metrics.
3. **Replicated users.** The 68,640 chains are 44 copies of 1,560 real student-terms.
   Copies of a class-term are treated as independent users, but their think-time sequences
   are identical up to a whole-week shift, so a quirk of one student is reproduced 44 times
   and the effective sample for anything driven by user behaviour is 1,560, not 68,640.
   The week-block bootstrap resamples time and does not cover this.
4. **The comparison stops being pathwise.** Section 8 says every policy sees the same
   arrival instants and the same service times, so the comparison is pathwise. Under gating
   each policy generates its own arrival sequence, so the closed-loop columns compare
   different sample paths over the same job population. The per-job guarantee is unaffected
   - it is stated against FCFS on the *realised* sequence, which is exactly what is
   computed here - but the gap-closed comparison is no longer pathwise.
5. **The deadline window is a calendar object and jobs drift out of it.** Under gating
   0.44 % to 1.35 % of submissions arrive after their own assignment's end, against 0.34 %
   in the recorded trace. We report both window definitions and both edge treatments and
   they move the p99 by under 1.3 %, but the choice is ours and not the data's.
6. **The chain definition is a choice.** A predecessor is the same student's previous
   submission in any exercise of any assessment. A referee could ask for per-exercise
   chains, for session boundaries in the sense of Zakay and Feitelson (a gap threshold), or
   for a partly-open model with a session-arrival process on top. Principle (vii) of
   Schroeder et al. says the answer depends on the session length, and our sessions are the
   whole term, which is the most closed reading available.
7. **One development pool, one platform.** Five overlays are week-shifts of six
   class-terms; the sealed terms are untouched. Nothing here transfers to the CI pools, the
   serverless trace or the compute farm, where per-user chains are not the right dependency
   structure.

## 8. Compute

package and the per-cell summary statistics dominate the rest.
